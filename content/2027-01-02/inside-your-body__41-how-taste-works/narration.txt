You bite into a lemon and your whole face scrunches up. How does your tongue tell your brain that?

Taste is your body's quality check for food and drink. The Dana Foundation says taste helps you decide whether to swallow something or spit out something harmful. It is a tiny guard standing at the door.

The guards live in your taste buds. Most of them sit on little bumps on the upper surface of your tongue called papillae, while others are on the roof of your mouth and the back of your throat.

How many do you have? BrainFacts, from the Society for Neuroscience, says each person has roughly five thousand to ten thousand taste buds. Other sources round the figure to about ten thousand. Either way, it is plenty.

Zoom in on a single taste bud and it looks like a tiny flower bud. Inside are about fifty to one hundred specialized sensory cells, and those cells are the ones that actually detect what you eat and drink.

Each of those taste cells responds to one of five basic tastes. They are sweet, salty, sour, bitter, and umami, which is a savory, meaty taste you find in foods like broth, cheese, and mushrooms.

Let us meet them one at a time. Sweet is the taste of sugar, like a ripe strawberry or a spoonful of honey. Most people find it pleasant right away.

Salty is the taste of table salt, like a pretzel. Sour is the sharp, mouth-puckering taste of a lemon. Bitter is the taste of things like strong black coffee or dark leafy greens. Each one reaches your brain as its own signal, so you can tell them apart in a single bite.

Umami completes the set. It gives a food that deep, savory, mouth-filling quality, like a good soup. Harvard Health lists it as the fifth taste, usually called savory.

Now for a famous idea that turns out to be wrong. Maybe you learned in school that the tongue has a map, with sweet at the tip, bitter at the back, and sour and salty along the sides. It is a myth.

BrainFacts explains where the myth began. A German scientist named David Hänig measured small differences in how strongly parts of the tongue responded, in a paper from 1901. Those differences were real, but small.

In the nineteen forties, a psychologist named Edwin Boring simplified the idea into a neat figure with one taste per region. It was easy to remember, so it ended up in textbooks, and the myth stuck around for decades.

The truth, as a neuroscientist at the Monell Chemical Senses Center put it, is that all regions of the tongue that detect taste respond to all five tastes. Place a drop of anything sweet anywhere on your tongue, and you will taste it.

There are small differences. Harvard Health says taste buds are more numerous along the sides of your tongue than down the middle, and bitter-sensitive buds are especially concentrated at the back. But those are matters of degree, not separate zones.

So what does the tongue send to the brain? Taste signals travel up to a brain region called the gustatory cortex. There they are combined with other senses, like smell, to create what you experience as flavor.

And here is the big twist. Much of what you think you taste is actually smell. The NIDCD explains that when you chew, aromas travel through a channel from the back of your mouth up to your nose.

Try a quick experiment. Pinch your nose closed while you chew a jelly bean. You will notice sweetness, but the cherry or lime stays hidden. Let go of your nose, and the flavor suddenly floods in.

This is also why food tastes bland when you have a cold. According to the NIDCD, a cold or flu can block the passage that carries odors from your throat to your nose, and food seems flat because flavor depends so much on smell.

So your tongue handles five basic tastes, and your nose adds the rest. Together with texture and temperature, your brain builds the huge range of flavors you enjoy, from tomato soup to chocolate.

Next question: why do you like some tastes and dislike others? Part of the answer is built in. Because taste helps you decide what to swallow and what to spit out, strong signals can feel pleasant or off-putting right away.

Many people find bitter foods harsh at first, and scientists think this may be one reason the sense exists, as a warning flag. That is an idea, though, not a rule. Plenty of bitter foods, like broccoli, are perfectly healthy, and many people learn to love them.

Experience matters a great deal too. BrainFacts quotes neuroscientist Steven Munger, who says our preferences, experiences, expectations, and motivations can all change how we feel about a taste.

That is why, for instance, people who grew up eating spicy food may love a hot pepper, while someone else finds it painful. Same tongue, same five tastes, different history, and the brain learns to enjoy what it gets used to.

Your sense of taste also changes with time. Harvard Health says taste may decline after age fifty, so grandma's love of extra salt and spice may not be just stubbornness.

Another thing that can change taste is medicine. Harvard Health notes that hundreds of medicines can alter taste. If a prescription makes everything taste strange, ask your doctor or pharmacist before changing anything yourself.

When should you actually get checked? If your sense of taste or smell drops and stays low, or it comes with other new symptoms, talk with your doctor. The NIDCD says a smell disorder can sometimes be an early sign of other health conditions.

Let us pull it together. Your taste buds sense five basic tastes, all across your tongue. Your nose adds most of the flavor. And your brain mixes in memory and habit to decide what you love.

So the next time you take a bite of something delicious, remember the team. Thousands of taste buds, a nose behind the scenes, and a brain that learned what you like, all working together in a single second.

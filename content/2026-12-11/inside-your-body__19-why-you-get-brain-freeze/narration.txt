One big gulp of a frozen slushy, and suddenly a stab of pain hits your forehead. Why does cold in your mouth hurt your head?

That is brain freeze. Doctors also call it an ice cream headache, a cold stimulus headache, or, if they want to impress you, sphenopalatine ganglioneuralgia. Try saying that with a mouth full of ice cream.

That long name points to a bundle of nerve cells tucked deep behind your nose. But the most interesting thing about brain freeze is not its name. It is that scientists have actually tested it, on purpose, in the lab.

So instead of just describing it, let us walk through the experiments. First, the basics that headache doctors agree on. Then two real studies, one with grown up volunteers, and one with a classroom full of kids and ice cream.

The International Headache Society officially classifies brain freeze as a real headache type. It is triggered when something cold, a food, a drink, or even cold air, passes over the roof of your mouth or the back of your throat.

The pain usually lands in your forehead or temples, most often on both sides. It comes on right after the cold hits. And by definition, it fades within ten minutes after the cold is gone.

In real life, it is usually much shorter. Cleveland Clinic says a brain freeze typically lasts a few seconds to two minutes, and it goes away on its own without any medicine.

Common triggers include ice cream, frozen and slushy drinks, ice pops, ice cubes, and breathing freezing air. Headache experts note that gulping crushed ice slush is especially likely to cause one. But even slow ice cream can.

Experiment one. In 2012, a team from Harvard Medical School and the National University of Ireland Galway wanted to watch a brain freeze happen. They recruited thirteen healthy adults.

The volunteers sipped ice water through a straw pressed against the roof of the mouth, the perfect spot to trigger brain freeze. Later they sipped the same amount of room temperature water, as a comparison.

Meanwhile, the scientists used ultrasound to measure blood flow in arteries in the brain. Volunteers raised a hand when the pain started, and raised it again when the pain faded. Simple, but clever.

Here is what they saw. Right as the pain began, an artery near the front of the brain, called the anterior cerebral artery, suddenly widened and sent a rush of blood toward the brain.

And as the pain faded, the same artery narrowed again. Pain on, artery wide. Pain off, artery back to normal. The timing matched closely, which suggests blood flow plays a big part in that sudden ache.

Why would your body do that? The lead researcher suggested that the brain is very sensitive to temperature. Widening the artery may rush warm blood in to protect it from the sudden chill in your mouth.

But there is a catch. Your skull is a closed box. Pushing in extra blood quickly could raise the pressure inside, and that pressure may be what hurts. Narrowing the artery afterward may let the pressure drop again.

Cleveland Clinic describes it in a similar way. Your body senses extreme cold in your mouth or throat, blood vessels in your head expand to bring in warmth, and the rapid change in their size causes the pain.

So why does it feel like your forehead and not your mouth? Scientists think it is a wiring mix up. Feeling from your face, the roof of your mouth and those head blood vessels runs largely through one big nerve, the trigeminal nerve.

When the alarm comes in, your brain cannot quite tell where it started, so it reports pain in your forehead. Doctors call this referred pain. It is a bit like a phone call that shows the wrong caller's name.

Experiment two. Back in 2002, two researchers in Canada ran a very tasty trial with one hundred forty five eighth grade students. Every student got the same scoop of ice cream, about one hundred milliliters.

Half the students had to finish it in under five seconds. The other half had to take more than thirty seconds. Then everyone answered a simple question: did you get an ice cream headache?

The results were clear. Twenty seven percent of the fast eaters got a brain freeze, compared with thirteen percent of the slow eaters. Eating fast roughly doubled the chances.

A few more details stood out. Most of those headaches lasted less than ten seconds. And when asked about their lives so far, seventy nine percent of the students said they had felt a brain freeze before.

Slowing down helped, but it was not a perfect shield. Some slow eaters still got brain freeze, and the whole study was run in the middle of a Canadian winter, when the air itself was cold.

Cleveland Clinic notes that children may get brain freeze more often, simply because they tend to dive into cold treats fast. And research suggests it is more common in people who get migraines.

That migraine link is why scientists care about something this silly. Because brain freeze is so easy to trigger safely and fades fast, it lets researchers study how a headache starts, which is very hard to catch with other headaches.

Now, the practical part. How do you stop one? Stop eating or drinking the cold thing. Then warm the roof of your mouth. Cleveland Clinic suggests pressing your tongue or your thumb against it.

Sipping a warm or room temperature drink helps too. To prevent brain freeze, eat and drink very cold things slowly, and in freezing weather, cover your mouth and nose with a scarf.

Brain freeze is harmless and does not need medical care. But if you get frequent headaches that last a while, or a headache that feels different from your usual, talk to a doctor.

So the next stab of brain freeze is your body defending your brain from a sudden chill. A widening artery, a confused nerve, and a few seconds of pain. Just slow down, put your tongue up, and enjoy the ice cream.

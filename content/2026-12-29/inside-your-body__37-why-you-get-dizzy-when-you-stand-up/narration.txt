You jump off the couch, and for a second the room goes fuzzy. Nothing is wrong with you. So what just happened?

That moment has a name, orthostatic response, and your body handles it every time you stand without you noticing. When it works, you feel nothing. When it lags, you feel that head rush.

To understand it, picture your blood as water in a tall pipe. Lying down, the pipe is flat, so the water spreads evenly. Stand up, and gravity starts pulling everything toward the bottom.

Your body has a lot of blood in its veins, which are soft, stretchy vessels. In fact, the venous system holds about seventy percent of your blood at any given time.

So when you stand, gravity pulls that blood downward. In a healthy person, about ten to fifteen percent of the blood volume, roughly seven hundred milliliters, can pool in the legs.

Seven hundred milliliters is a little over two cans of soda. And about half of that shift happens in just the first twenty to thirty seconds after you stand up.

The result is that less blood flows back to your heart. With less blood coming in, the heart pumps out less with each beat, and your blood pressure starts to drop. Your brain notices first.

But you do not usually faint, because your body has a built-in alarm system. In the walls of your aorta and the carotid arteries in your neck are pressure sensors called baroreceptors.

When you stand and pressure falls, those sensors get stretched less, and they send quieter signals to your brainstem. Your brain reads this quiet as a message: pressure is dropping, act now.

The fastest response comes first. Your brain eases off the brakes it normally keeps on your heart, which makes your heart rate jump within moments. It is like pressing down on the gas pedal.

Next, a slower signal arrives through the sympathetic nerves. This one speeds your heart further and tells small blood vessels to squeeze, which raises the resistance blood has to push against.

Think of squeezing the end of a garden hose. The water inside gets more pressure. That is what your vessels do, and it pushes your blood pressure back up toward where it started.

Your leg muscles help too. Every time they tighten, they squeeze the veins and push blood back up toward your heart. That is why a little fidgeting or shifting your weight can help.

So why does it sometimes slip? If you stand very fast, your body has less time to catch up. The pooling happens quickly, and the response needs a moment to cover the gap.

That gap is the head rush. You might feel lightheaded, see your vision blur or dim at the edges, or feel a bit unsteady for a few seconds. It usually fades as your pressure catches up.

Some situations make it worse. Heat, like a hot shower, lets vessels widen. Being low on fluids means less blood to go around. And a big meal sends blood to your gut, so the effect can show up after eating.

It tends to be worse in the morning, too, after a night without water. Cleveland Clinic also notes that teenagers in growth spurts and people who have recently had a baby can notice it more.

It also becomes more common with age. With the years, the walls of the arteries holding those sensors can stiffen, and the heart rate response gets weaker, so the fix works a little slower.

Doctors have a rule for when this crosses a line. Orthostatic hypotension means your systolic blood pressure, the top number, falls by more than twenty within three minutes of standing, or the bottom number falls by more than ten.

Some people with this have no symptoms at all. Others feel lightheaded, weak, or foggy. Causes include dehydration, long bed rest, some medicines, and conditions of the heart, nerves, or hormones, so it is worth getting checked.

Now, practical tips. The NHS suggests getting up slowly from sitting or lying down. Sit on the edge of the bed for a moment first, so your body can start adjusting before you stand.

Research in older adults found that standing up slowly helped soften the first drop in blood pressure during the opening fifteen seconds. A little patience really does help.

Drinking enough water also helps, and so do smaller meals and limiting alcohol. Cleveland Clinic adds a simple trick: if you must stand for a long time, march your feet to keep blood moving.

Another tip: before you stand, clench your hands or squeeze a ball for a few seconds. That small effort can help raise blood pressure a bit. And always hold something sturdy if you feel wobbly.

Now the important part: when to see a doctor. Cleveland Clinic says to contact a provider if you often feel dizzy when you stand, even if it only happens some of the time.

The NHS says the same: see a doctor if dizziness or fainting keeps happening. A quick blood pressure check, lying and then standing, can tell your doctor a lot.

Get urgent care if you faint, fall, or hurt your head, or if dizziness comes with chest pain, trouble with balance, or signs like cold, clammy skin and a weak pulse. Call emergency services in those cases.

And please do not stop or change any medicine on your own because of dizziness. Talk to your doctor first, who can adjust it safely if that is the cause.

So the head rush is not a flaw. It is a lag in an amazing system: gravity pulls, sensors shout, your heart speeds up, and your vessels squeeze, all in a few seconds.

Next time the room tilts for a moment, wait it out, smile, and thank your baroreceptors. They are hard at work every single time you stand up.

Take more than the mass of the Sun. Crush it into a ball the size of a city. Now let's go inside.

A neutron star is what's left when a giant star dies. Its core collapses in about a second, so violently that the outer layers are blasted away in a supernova. What remains is the densest thing in the universe that still has a surface.

A typical one holds about one point four times the mass of the Sun, or half a million Earths. And it fits into a sphere only about twelve miles across. NASA compares it to the length of Manhattan. You could drive across it in about fifteen minutes, if there were a road.

That makes it about a hundred trillion times denser than water. A piece of neutron star the size of a sugar cube would weigh about a billion tons. A teaspoon would outweigh every human being on Earth put together.

To picture that density, imagine squeezing the entire Earth just as hard. Every ocean, mountain, and city, the whole planet, would shrink into a ball only about as wide as the Empire State Building is tall.

Why neutrons? Under that crushing pressure, electrons are forced into protons, and each pair becomes a neutron. In a sense, the whole star is like one giant atomic nucleus, held together not by nuclear forces, but by its own gravity.

Before we go inside, let's approach from space. The first thing you'd notice is that it's spinning. Most neutron stars rotate anywhere from once every few seconds to hundreds of times per second. As they turn, beams of radiation sweep past like a lighthouse. We call those pulsars.

The record holder, found with the Green Bank Telescope in West Virginia, spins seven hundred sixteen times every second. It's no more than about twenty miles across, which means its equator is moving at a sizable fraction of the speed of light. Spin much faster, and theory says it could start to fly apart.

Next, the magnetism. A typical neutron star's magnetic field is trillions of times stronger than Earth's. A special kind, called a magnetar, is a thousand times stronger still. Neutron stars are the strongest magnets in the known universe.

And magnetars can erupt. In two thousand four, a magnetar called SGR eighteen oh six minus twenty flared for about a tenth of a second. In that instant, it released more energy than our Sun has given off in the last hundred thousand years. It sits tens of thousands of light-years away, yet the blast was still detected here.

Now come closer. Gravity is so intense here that it bends light around the star. Looking at it, you would see not just the half facing you, but part of the back side as well. The star looks bigger than it really is.

At the surface, gravity is more than a hundred billion times stronger than on Earth. To escape it, you'd need to travel more than half the speed of light. Drop a marshmallow from about three feet up, and it would slam into the ground at millions of miles per hour.

But that's not the strangest part. That gravity flattens everything. The atmosphere is a layer of hydrogen or helium just a couple of centimeters high. And the tallest mountains? A study from the University of Southampton found they'd be only a fraction of a millimeter tall.

Time to dig. Just below the surface lies the outer crust. Here the pressure is so great that atoms are stripped of their electrons. What's left is a rigid lattice of atomic nuclei, mostly iron and similar elements, swimming in a sea of free electrons. It's a solid, crushed far beyond anything on Earth.

Go deeper, into the inner crust, and something strange begins. The nuclei become so crowded and so overloaded with neutrons that neutrons start leaking out of them. Now the solid lattice is soaked in a fluid of free neutrons, flowing between the nuclei.

Near the bottom of the crust, the nuclei can't stay round anymore. Physicists think they stretch and merge into long rods and flat sheets. They have a delicious name for it: nuclear pasta. The rods are spaghetti. The sheets are lasagna.

And this pasta is anything but soft. In twenty eighteen, physicists from McGill, Indiana University, and Caltech ran huge computer simulations to see how hard it would be to break. Their conclusion: nuclear pasta may be the strongest material in the known universe.

Here is where the crust ends, maybe half a mile to a mile down. Below it, there are no nuclei left at all. Welcome to the outer core. The pressure has squeezed most of the protons and electrons together into neutrons, leaving a dense sea of mostly neutrons.

Scientists think this neutron sea behaves as a superfluid, a liquid with zero friction, able to flow without losing energy. If you could stir it, it would never stop swirling. It's the same kind of quantum behavior we can only create in labs at temperatures near absolute zero, yet here it exists inside a star.

There may even be a way to watch that superfluid at work. Every few years, the Vela pulsar suddenly spins a tiny bit faster, an event astronomers call a glitch. A leading idea is that the swirling superfluid inside briefly grabs hold of the crust and gives it a kick.

Now we reach the very center, and here's the honest truth. Nobody knows what's down there. It is the one place in the universe where matter is crushed harder than anything we can recreate on Earth. Physicists have several competing ideas.

Maybe it's neutrons all the way down, packed tighter and tighter. Maybe the neutrons themselves break apart into their smaller building blocks, called quarks, forming a soup of free quarks. Or maybe those quarks recombine into exotic particles we have only glimpsed in particle accelerators.

How do you test ideas about a place you can never visit? You measure the star's size. Softer, more squeezable matter makes a smaller star. Stiffer matter makes a bigger one. So NASA put an X-ray telescope called NICER on the International Space Station to do exactly that.

NICER times the X-ray flashes from hot spots on spinning neutron stars, and uses the way gravity bends that light to work out their size. One star it studied holds about one point four Suns and is roughly sixteen miles across.

Then came a surprise. A second star holds about two point one Suns, fifty percent more mass, yet it's about the same size. Adding all that mass barely squeezed it. That tells physicists the matter inside is stiffer than some theories predicted, and it challenges models where the star is made only of neutrons.

So far, we've been looking at just one neutron star. But our galaxy is full of them. Scientists estimate the Milky Way may hold hundreds of millions, most of them old, cold, and too faint to see. And sometimes, two of them meet.

And here's the most surprising part. These dead stars don't always stay dead. Some come in pairs, orbiting each other, slowly spiraling inward as they lose energy. Eventually they collide.

In August twenty seventeen, gravitational wave detectors felt exactly that: two neutron stars merging about a hundred thirty million light-years away. Around seventy observatories then turned to look, and saw the glowing debris of the crash, a kind of explosion called a kilonova.

Inside that debris, astronomers found the signatures of freshly made heavy elements, including gold and platinum. Neutron-rich matter, flung out of the collision, builds heavy atoms in seconds. That's why many astronomers think collisions like this are a major source of the universe's gold.

So let's put it all together. You'd start at a surface smoother than a billiard ball, under an atmosphere about as thick as your finger. You'd sink through a crust of bare nuclei, past rivers of free neutrons, through pasta stronger than anything else known, into a frictionless neutron sea.

And at the bottom, a core that might hold a form of matter no one has ever seen. All of that, packed into a ball you could walk across in an afternoon, if the gravity didn't flatten you first.

Next time you see a gold ring or a gold coin, think about where those atoms may have come from. Very possibly, from the collision of two of these impossible city-sized stars, billions of years before the Earth was born.

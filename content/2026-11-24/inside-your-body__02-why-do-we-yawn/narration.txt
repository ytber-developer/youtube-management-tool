You might yawn just watching this. Scientists still can't fully explain why we do it, but they do have some strong suspects.

A yawn is a whole routine. You open your jaw wide, take a long, deep breath, pause, then breathe out. It usually lasts four to seven seconds, and the average adult yawns about nine times a day.

The oldest idea says yawning brings in extra oxygen. It sounds logical, but it failed the test. In a 1987 experiment, people breathed air with extra oxygen or extra carbon dioxide, and their yawning didn't change.

The leading idea today is brain cooling. A deep yawn pulls in cooler air and shifts blood flow through your head, a bit like a radiator. In rats, brain temperature rose just before a yawn and dropped right after.

There's support in people, too. In Tucson, Arizona, pedestrians were nearly twice as likely to yawn in winter as on scorching summer days, when the outside air was about as warm as their bodies and couldn't cool much.

Then there's the strangest part. Yawns are contagious. Seeing or hearing someone yawn can set you off, and you're more likely to catch one from family and friends than from strangers. Many researchers link this to empathy.

And yawning starts before you're even born. Babies yawn in the womb about three months into pregnancy. That's one reason the oxygen idea never quite fit: those babies aren't breathing air at all.

So why do we yawn? Probably for more than one reason: cooling the brain, nudging it awake, maybe keeping a group in sync. The honest answer is that science is still working on it.

What if you could jump on every planet in the solar system? Where would you fly the highest, and where would you never come down?

Let's make it a game with fair rules. On Earth, you crouch and leap, and your feet rise exactly one foot off the ground. Then you take that very same jump, with the very same push, to every world.

A jump like that launches you at about five and a half miles per hour. What happens next depends only on gravity. Weaker gravity, and you rise higher and hang longer. Stronger gravity, and you get pulled back down fast.

On Earth, that one-foot jump keeps you in the air for about half a second. One foot, half a second: that's our scoreboard. And let's give you a magic suit, because almost everywhere we're going would kill you in moments.

First stop, Mercury, the planet closest to the Sun. Gravity here is about thirty-eight percent of Earth's. Your jump soars to more than two and a half feet, and you hang in the air for over a second.

Just pick your landing time carefully. With almost no air to hold heat, Mercury's surface can reach about eight hundred degrees Fahrenheit by day, then plunge to around minus two hundred ninety at night.

Next, Venus. Its gravity is about ninety percent of ours, so your jump barely changes. You rise roughly thirteen inches, just a touch higher than at home. The jump is easy. Surviving the landing is the hard part.

The surface of Venus is about eight hundred seventy degrees Fahrenheit, the hottest of any planet. And the thick air presses down on you as hard as the water would if you were more than three thousand feet under the ocean.

Soviet Venera landers made it to that surface. The toughest one kept working for about two hours before the heat and pressure won. The shortest lasted only twenty-three minutes. So on Venus, jump quickly.

Quick detour: our Moon. It isn't a planet, but it's the only other world where people have actually jumped. With about one sixth of Earth's gravity, your jump rises around six feet and lasts about three seconds.

That's why Apollo astronauts bounced instead of walking. Even weighed down by bulky suits and backpacks, they hopped across the gray dust like kangaroos in slow motion, sometimes tumbling over in the dust.

Now Mars, and our first surprise. Mars is much bigger than Mercury, yet its gravity is basically the same, about thirty-eight percent of Earth's. Your jump ties with Mercury: more than two and a half feet.

The secret is density. Mercury is packed far more tightly than Mars, roughly forty percent denser. A small, heavy ball can pull on you just as hard at its surface as a larger, lighter one.

Mars does have air, but barely. Its surface pressure is less than one percent of Earth's, so real explorers will still need pressurized suits. But every one of their steps will feel light and springy.

Next, the king, Jupiter. Problem number one: there's nowhere to stand. Jupiter has no solid surface, just thick clouds over swirling gas and liquid that get hotter and denser the deeper you sink.

So let's cheat and jump from the cloud tops, where scientists measure its gravity. It's about two and a half times Earth's. Your one-foot jump shrinks to about five inches, and it's over in roughly a fifth of a second.

You'd feel it in your knees, too. A one hundred fifty pound person would weigh around three hundred seventy-five pounds there. And if you sank in, NASA says the pressure and heat would crush, melt and vaporize any spacecraft.

Saturn is next, with surprise number two. Saturn has about ninety-five times Earth's mass. Yet at its cloud tops, your jump would go slightly higher than on Earth, about thirteen inches.

How? Saturn is so wide that its cloud tops sit very far from its center, and it is so light for its size that it's the only planet less dense than water. In a big enough bathtub, NASA says, it would float.

On to Uranus, the planet that rolls around the Sun on its side, tilted about ninety-eight degrees. Its gravity is a bit weaker than ours as well. Your jump: about thirteen and a half inches, the best among the giants.

Don't linger, though. Parts of the Uranian atmosphere drop to about minus three hundred seventy degrees Fahrenheit, among the coldest temperatures found on any planet. Even inside a magic suit, that sounds unpleasant.

Neptune is a little heavier on your legs. Its gravity is about twelve percent stronger than ours, trimming your jump to just under eleven inches. And you would have to time it carefully, between gusts.

Neptune has the strongest winds in the solar system, reaching more than twelve hundred miles per hour. That's nearly five times faster than the most powerful winds on Earth. One jump, and you're gone sideways.

Time to check the scoreboard. Worst jump: Jupiter, about five inches. Earth, Venus, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune all land within two inches of a foot. And Mercury and Mars tie for the lead at over two and a half feet.

But we're not done. Pluto, the dwarf planet beyond Neptune, has gravity only about six percent of Earth's. Your one-foot jump becomes roughly sixteen feet. You would sail right past the rim of a basketball hoop.

And you'd drift up and back down for about eight seconds. Plenty of time to admire mountains made of water ice, plains of frozen nitrogen, and the famous bright heart that NASA's New Horizons photographed in 2015.

What about the Sun itself? Ignore the fact that you'd be vaporized instantly. Its surface gravity is about twenty-eight times Earth's, so your mighty jump would lift you less than half an inch.

Now the grand finale: a comet. In 2014, the European Space Agency landed a probe called Philae on comet sixty-seven P. Its harpoons failed to fire as it touched down, and the lander bounced.

Rising at a gentle thirty-eight centimeters per second, less than one mile per hour, Philae sailed about a kilometer across the comet and stayed aloft for an hour and fifty minutes before touching down again.

Scientists estimate the comet's escape speed at only about half a meter per second, roughly one mile per hour. Your jump launches you at about five and a half miles per hour. That's around five times more than you need.

So on a comet, your ordinary one-foot hop would never bring you back down. You would drift off into space and keep going. The best jump in the whole solar system is the one that never ends.

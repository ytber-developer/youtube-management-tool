You are sitting in a silent meeting when your stomach lets out a long, loud growl. Why does your belly do that?

That embarrassing sound has an official name, borborygmi. Say it slowly. Bor, bo, rig, me. And doctors actually listen for these sounds on purpose, with a stethoscope pressed against your belly.

Let's treat this like a mystery. We have a suspect, the stomach. We have a motive, hunger. And we have the evidence, that noise. But as we follow the clues, each one gets more surprising.

Clue number one. What makes the sound? Harvard Health says the exact source of bowel sounds is uncertain, but they are thought to come from gas moving around, pushed by the muscular walls of your gut.

Your digestive tract is not a passive tube. Its walls are muscle that squeezes in an organized, step by step wave. Doctors call this peristalsis, and it keeps food moving along.

As those waves squeeze, they often mix air and liquid together. Push gas and fluid through a narrow, bendy tube, and you get gurgles, rumbles, and the occasional long growl.

Clue number two. Where is it really coming from? MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, describes abdominal sounds as the noises made by the intestines as food is pushed through.

So the stomach is not the only suspect. Your intestines are long, coiled, and busy, and the sounds from your stomach and intestines blend together. The growl may be coming from several places at once.

Clue number three. Is it really hunger? Here is a twist. Your gut makes noise after meals too. MedlinePlus says bowel sounds can become more active after eating, sometimes loud enough to hear without a stethoscope.

So a growl is not always a hunger alarm. Digesting a big meal can be noisy business. But there is a special kind of rumble that shows up when you have not eaten for a while.

To understand it, meet the migrating motor complex. It is a repeating pattern of muscle activity in your stomach and small intestine that runs while you are fasting, and it stops when you eat.

Researchers divide it into four phases. Things begin quietly, then the muscles stir. Then comes phase three, the big one, a strong burst of contractions that sweeps down through your gut.

That powerful wave starts in your stomach or at the beginning of your small intestine and travels downward, like a squeeze passing along a tube of toothpaste. Then the whole cycle starts over again.

How often does it come? According to a review in the Journal of Smooth Muscle Research, these strong contractions return about every ninety to one hundred twenty minutes in people. Skip a meal, and your gut keeps its own schedule.

So what is this wave for? The same review describes it as cleaning. It sweeps the empty stomach clean, mechanically and chemically, to get it ready for your next meal.

Think of it like a crew that tidies the kitchen after everyone has gone to bed. It works on its own timetable, whether or not you are thinking about food.

And when a strong wave squeezes an empty stomach that holds mostly gas and fluid, scientists think it can make quite a racket. That is often the growl you hear hours after a meal.

Your stomach's cleaning wave also takes orders from the vagus nerve, a long nerve linking your brain and your gut. The Deloose review notes that when this nerve is cut, the stomach's wave stops, but the small intestine keeps its own rhythm.

The brain connection may explain another clue. In experiments with dogs, stress from loud noise weakened the stomach's cleaning wave, while the intestine's wave carried on. Your mood and your gut are more linked than you might think.

Clue number four is the real surprise. What sets off that cleaning wave in your stomach? A gut hormone called motilin, which helps regulate this whole fasting cycle.

In a 2016 study in the journal Gut, researchers tracked hunger in fasting volunteers. Hunger ratings were higher during the strong stomach phase of the cleaning cycle than during the quieter phases.

Then they gave a drug that acts like motilin. It set off an early stomach wave, and hunger scores jumped along with it. Motilin, not the better known hunger hormone ghrelin, produced that effect.

The researchers concluded that this motilin driven stomach wave is a hunger signal from the gut. So the growl and the hunger can come from the same event, your stomach clearing out for the next meal.

They noticed something else. In patients with unexplained loss of appetite, that strong stomach phase was missing, and their hunger ratings were lower. It hints at how closely the two are linked.

Scientists think this cleaning wave matters for more than noise. When it goes missing, it has been linked to problems such as slow stomach emptying and an overgrowth of bacteria in the small intestine.

So, case closed? Mostly. The stomach is guilty of growling, but it has accomplices. Gas, fluid, your intestines, a cleaning cycle, and a hormone all play their part in the noise.

What about the quiet times? MedlinePlus notes that bowel sounds are normally reduced during sleep. Your gut is still working through the night, it just gets quieter for a while.

And sometimes the growling gets louder for a reason. Harvard Health says a stomach bug, like viral gastroenteritis, can make bowel sounds louder, high pitched, or even tinkling.

Here is the counterintuitive part. Doctors often worry more when a belly is silent than when it is noisy. If someone has belly pain and no bowel sounds at all, that can point to a serious problem.

So when should you see a doctor? MedlinePlus advises getting care for rectal bleeding, nausea, vomiting, or diarrhea or constipation that keeps going. Belly pain along with strange gut noises is a reason to get checked too.

But an ordinary rumble on its own, with no pain or other symptoms, is simply the sound of a healthy digestive system doing its job, right on schedule.

Can you quiet it down? A review in Nature Reviews Gastroenterology and Hepatology explains that the fasting cycle is interrupted by eating. That is why a snack often calms the rumbling.

So next time your stomach roars in a quiet room, you can smile. It is not just hunger. It is a scheduled cleaning wave, sweeping your stomach and telling your brain it is ready for more.

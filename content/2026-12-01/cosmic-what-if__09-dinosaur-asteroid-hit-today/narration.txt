Sixty-six million years ago, a rock the size of a city ended the age of dinosaurs. Now imagine it arrives this afternoon, same spot.

The rock is roughly six to seven miles wide. Set it down on the beach and its top would reach about as high as a jetliner cruises. It is moving around fifty times the speed of sound, fast enough to cross the United States in about four minutes.

Its target is the northern edge of Mexico's Yucatan Peninsula, near a small coastal town called Chicxulub. Today that coast has resort beaches, fishing harbors, and the city of Merida just inland. Sixty-six million years ago it was shallow sea, and the rock was coming in from the northeast.

Here's an unlucky detail. Crater studies from Imperial College London suggest it hit at a steep angle, about sixty degrees. That angle, the team found, throws out more climate-changing gas per ton of rock than a shallow or a straight-down hit. It may have been close to the deadliest angle possible.

The air in front of it cannot get out of the way. It squeezes, heats, and glows. Crossing the thickest part of the atmosphere takes only seconds, so no one along the Gulf Coast hears a thing before it strikes. They just see a light brighter than the Sun.

Impact. The rock punches a hole about eighteen miles deep into the Earth's crust, then mostly vaporizes. Granite from six miles down is flung upward at over twelve thousand miles per hour. Within minutes, the ground rebounds and settles into a crater more than a hundred miles across.

Researchers estimate that anyone within roughly nine hundred miles would be killed almost instantly by the fireball. Draw that circle on a map of today, and it reaches Havana, Mexico City, Miami, Houston, and New Orleans. Closer in, a wall of wind tears outward at more than six hundred miles per hour.

But the fireball is not the strangest part. The impact also kicks the ocean. Within about ten minutes, a ring-shaped wave more than a mile high is racing away from the crater. Scientists led by the University of Michigan have now simulated it across the whole planet.

In the open Gulf of Mexico, waves would still tower hundreds of feet high an hour after impact. The researchers estimate the tsunami started with up to thirty thousand times the energy of the two thousand four Indian Ocean tsunami, which killed more than two hundred thirty thousand people.

Within two days, the waves reach most of the world's coastlines. Far across the North Atlantic, the model still shows waves approaching thirty feet as they near the coast. The study didn't model the flooding on land, but every port and beach city in the region would be in the water's path.

We know this happened because the seafloor remembers. The team checked more than a hundred rock sites from before and after the impact and found the tsunami's scars, including in New Zealand, about seventy-five hundred miles away.

Thousands of miles north, in what is now North Dakota, a fossil site called Tanis seems to preserve the chaos of the first minutes. Ocean fish, freshwater fish, and logs are dumped together in a layer more than four feet thick, some fish with tiny beads of impact glass in their gills.

But the waves are not the worst of it either. Meanwhile, the sky is filling up. Vaporized rock, dust, and soot rise more than fifteen miles into the air and spread around the planet. Billions of tons of sulfur from the seafloor rocks join them. Within days, daylight fades almost everywhere.

And here's what turns a disaster into an extinction. Without sunlight, global temperatures drop by roughly thirty-six degrees Fahrenheit. Much of the land stays below freezing for about three years. The oceans stay chilled for centuries.

For the dinosaurs, the food chain simply collapsed. Plants stopped growing, plant eaters starved, and the hunters followed. For us, the same darkness hits every wheat field, cornfield, and rice paddy on the planet at once. There is no country that could grow its way through it.

That dust eventually settles into a thin layer of clay found all over the world, usually just a few inches thick, and rich in a metal called iridium that is rare in Earth's crust but common in asteroids. It is the asteroid itself, spread across the planet like a fingerprint.

In the end, about three quarters of all species on Earth disappeared. Almost no land animal heavier than about fifty-five pounds made it through. Every non-bird dinosaur was gone.

So who survived? Small animals that could burrow, hide, and live on almost nothing. Creatures on the ocean floor. Ferns, which tolerate darkness and were among the first plants to return. And our own ancestors, small furry mammals that could ride out the cold underground.

Here's the surprising part. Life came back fast. Inside the crater itself, tiny ocean organisms returned within about ten years. Within about thirty thousand years, a thriving ecosystem had moved in. Out of that recovery came the age of mammals, and eventually, you.

And the crater? You could stand on it today and never know. It is buried under more than half a mile of younger rock. The only clue at the surface is a curving ring of sinkholes, called cenotes, that traces the old rim through the jungle.

So could it happen again? NASA estimates there are around a thousand near-Earth asteroids bigger than about two thirds of a mile. About ninety-five percent of them have already been found, and none is a current threat. Something six miles wide would be even harder to miss.

And unlike the dinosaurs, we can push back. In twenty twenty-two, NASA slammed its DART spacecraft into a small asteroid about seven million miles away and changed its orbit by thirty-two minutes. The dinosaurs never saw it coming. We are learning to look up.

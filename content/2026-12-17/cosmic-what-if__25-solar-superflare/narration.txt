What if the Sun fired the biggest flare in human history straight at Earth today? Here's what your next few days would look like.

First, what is a solar flare? It's a sudden flash on the Sun, when tangled magnetic fields snap and release a burst of light and energy. A superflare is that same flash, turned up enormously.

How enormously? Astronomers say a superflare releases more energy than a trillion hydrogen bombs. The most famous solar storm on record, the Carrington Event of 1859, released only about a hundredth of that.

So let's start a clock. Minute zero: the flare erupts on the side of the Sun facing us. You don't feel a thing. You're making breakfast and scrolling your phone, totally unaware.

Minute eight. The flash arrives. Light takes about eight minutes to cross from the Sun to Earth, and the flare's energy travels at the speed of light. So there is no warning before this first wave hits.

Here's the good news first. You're safe on the ground. NASA says flares are not directly harmful to people on Earth, because our atmosphere soaks up most of the dangerous radiation before it reaches you.

But that soaking up has a side effect. The radiation electrifies the upper atmosphere over the daytime side of the planet. Shortwave radio, the kind ships and planes use over the oceans, starts to crackle and fail.

Less than an hour in, a second wave arrives: fast particles from the Sun, some racing at nearly light speed. On the ground, you feel nothing. Up in orbit, it's a different story.

NASA warns that this kind of radiation can be harmful to astronauts outside Earth's protective atmosphere. Crews on a space station would retreat to its most shielded corners and wait for the storm to pass.

Satellites take hits too. Fast particles can flip bits in computer memory and blind the cameras some spacecraft use to steer by the stars. Some satellites could glitch, reboot, or go quiet for good.

But the flare is only the opening act. Often a big flare comes with a coronal mass ejection, which can hurl billions of tons of magnetized gas off the Sun. That's the part that can reach our technology.

A slow cloud like that can take several days to reach Earth. The fastest arrive in as little as fifteen to eighteen hours. In 1859, the Carrington storm took about seventeen and a half.

So this time, you get a warning. A NOAA satellite called DSCOVR sits about a million miles from Earth, watching the solar wind. It can give forecasters a short heads-up before the cloud slams into us.

Hour eighteen. Impact. Earth's magnetic field gets squeezed and shaken like a struck bell. Scientists call this a geomagnetic storm, and a superflare could unleash the strongest one in recorded history.

The first thing you'd notice is beautiful. The sky lights up. In 1859, auroras were seen as far south as the Caribbean, and in the northeastern United States, people could read a newspaper outside at night.

Picture it: glowing curtains of red and green over cities that have never seen an aurora. People pour into the streets to take photos. For a few hours, it feels like a celebration.

But the same storm is doing something invisible. As the magnetic field shakes, it drives electric currents through the ground, and into anything long and metal: pipelines, railways, and above all, power lines.

We've had previews. In 1859, telegraph lines carried so much current that the equipment sparked, and operators reported fires. Telegraph paper went up in smoke. That network was the internet of its time.

And in 1989, a much weaker storm hit Quebec. Within ninety seconds, with no warning, six million people lost power. Most got it back in about nine hours, but many waited for days.

Now scale that up. The grid depends on giant transformers that weigh many tons. Extra current from a storm can overheat and damage them, and without them, power can't flow to your neighborhood.

Researchers studying a Carrington sized storm estimate that twenty to forty million Americans could lose power for a long time, anywhere from about sixteen days to as long as one or two years.

Day two. Your phone still turns on, but cell towers run on backup batteries and generators, and many could fade within days. Gas pumps need electricity. So do the plants that clean your water.

Navigation gets shaky too. Storms scramble the upper atmosphere that GPS signals pass through, so the blue dot on your map might drift. Radios used for search and rescue can fail at the same time.

Satellites in low orbit face one more problem. A storm heats and puffs up the upper atmosphere, adding drag. In 2022, a fairly minor storm doomed up to forty brand new Starlink satellites right after launch.

Add it all up and the bill is staggering. A National Academy of Sciences study estimated that a storm like Carrington could cost more than two trillion dollars, about twenty times the cost of Hurricane Katrina.

And this isn't science fiction. In July 2012, the Sun launched a storm at least as strong as the Carrington Event. It tore through Earth's orbit and missed us. One week earlier, and we'd have been in the line of fire.

Superflares may also be more common than we thought. In 2024, scientists studied more than fifty six thousand Sun-like stars watched by NASA's Kepler telescope. On average, such stars produced a superflare about once per century.

That doesn't mean our Sun is due. The study can't predict when, or even whether, our Sun will do it. But it suggests that extreme outbursts are a normal part of how stars like ours behave.

Earth may even keep a diary of past outbursts. In old tree rings, scientists found a sudden spike of radioactive carbon from around the year seven hundred seventy-four. Many researchers think a giant blast from the Sun caused it, though it's still debated.

Back then, it hardly mattered. There were no power grids, no satellites, no phones. A storm like that meant a strange, glowing sky and little else. Today, we've wired the entire planet.

So what protects us? Warnings. Spacecraft watch the Sun around the clock, and a heads-up gives grid operators time to protect equipment. In 2031, Europe plans to launch a spacecraft called Vigil to improve those forecasts.

And for you, the smart move is simple and a little boring: the same kit you'd keep for a hurricane. Water, food, a flashlight, spare batteries, some cash, and a paper map.

So if a superflare hit today, it probably wouldn't hurt you directly. You'd see the most spectacular sky of your life. The danger is to the invisible web of wires and satellites that modern life runs on.

Sooner or later, the Sun will likely send another giant storm our way. The real question is whether our planet will be ready when it does.

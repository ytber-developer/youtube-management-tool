You close your eyes at eleven and open them at seven. Nothing seems to happen. But your brain just ran a busy show. What happens?

For a long time, people assumed sleep was just a switch. Brain off, rest, brain on. We now know it is an organized sequence, with different stages that each do different jobs, repeating through the night.

Let's follow you through one night. Your head hits the pillow, the lights go out, and you start to drift. Sleep scientists divide what happens next into two big phases, called non-REM and REM.

The first phase is non-REM sleep, and it has three stages. Stage one is the doorway. It is the transition from being awake to being asleep, and it is the lightest stage. Your brain is just starting to let go of the day.

Then comes stage two. According to the National Heart, Lung, and Blood Institute, this is the stage where you are actually asleep. You spend a lot of the night here, quietly, without any drama.

And then you sink into stage three, deep sleep, also called slow-wave sleep. The name comes from the slow, large waves that show up on brain recordings. This stage is more common early in the night.

And it is busy. A major review of sleep and memory says that during slow-wave sleep, recently stored memories are reactivated. The brain appears to replay patterns of activity from the day.

That replay seems to help move memories from a temporary holding area into longer term storage. Scientists call it system consolidation. So deep sleep is a bit like your brain's filing time.

Here is a helpful way to think about it. The review describes the waking brain as built for taking in new information, and the sleeping brain as built for locking that information in. Each does what it does best.

Now the main event, at least for dreams. After a while, you move into R E M sleep. R E M stands for rapid eye movement, because under your closed lids, your eyes dart quickly back and forth.

During R E M, brain activity looks surprisingly similar to being awake. This is the stage when dreaming usually happens, and your body does something clever. Your muscles go limp, which keeps you from acting out your dreams.

The review suggests R E M sleep may help stabilize memories after they have been transformed by deep sleep. Think of it as deep sleep drafting the notes, and R E M helping them stick.

But scientists add a note of humility. Older research focused on R E M sleep, newer work stressed deep sleep, and the exact roles of each stage are still being worked out. Memory is a team effort.

Now here is the pattern that matters. You do not go through these stages once. You cycle through them. The NHLBI says each cycle lasts about eighty to one hundred minutes, which is roughly ninety, with four to six cycles on most nights.

So your night is a loop. Light sleep, deeper sleep, deep sleep, R E M, then back again. You may wake briefly between cycles, and most of the time you will not remember it at all.

The mix changes as the night goes on. Early cycles have more deep sleep. Later cycles have more R E M. So the first half of the night leans toward deep sleep, and the second half leans toward dreaming.

That explains a familiar feeling. If you wake up at five and cannot get back to sleep, you may miss part of the later, R E M-heavy stretch of the night, even if the early hours went fine.

Here is another clue that sleep matters. Researchers point out that sleep is found across all animal species, which suggests it serves a vital function. Something that important rarely survives evolution by accident.

Next, the brain's cleaning crew. In 2013, a team at the University of Rochester studied mice and reported that during natural sleep, the space between brain cells grew by about sixty percent.

That extra room let cerebrospinal fluid, the clear liquid that surrounds the brain, flow through more easily and wash through the tissue. In the mice, this sped up the removal of a protein waste product called beta-amyloid.

The researchers suggested that sleep's restorative function may be, in part, the removal of waste products that build up while the brain is awake. That cleanup system is often called the glymphatic system.

Now the honest caveat. That was a study in mice. How much of it works the same way in people, and how big the effect is, is still being studied. So treat it as an exciting idea, not a final answer.

Even with those caveats, the big picture is clear. The CDC says good sleep is essential for health and emotional well-being, and that enough quality sleep may help you get sick less often and manage stress.

The CDC also notes that enough sleep may improve attention and memory, support heart and metabolic health, and lower the risk of conditions like type two diabetes, high blood pressure, and stroke.

How much sleep is enough? CDC guidance says adults aged eighteen to sixty need seven or more hours a night. For ages sixty-one to sixty-four it is seven to nine hours, and for sixty-five and older, seven to eight.

Notice why hours matter. With four to six cycles per night, cutting your sleep short can trim the final cycles, which are rich in R E M. Seven hours is a bit like staying for the whole show, though the exact effects vary from person to person.

So what about the nights when sleep just will not cooperate? Everyone has a rough night now and then, and it is usually nothing to worry about. A single bad night does not wreck your brain.

But the CDC says to talk with a healthcare provider if you regularly have trouble sleeping, or notice signs of a sleep disorder. Insomnia, restless legs, narcolepsy, and sleep apnea are all common examples.

A provider may ask you to keep a sleep diary, noting bedtimes, waking times, naps, exercise, alcohol, caffeine, and medicines. They may also order a sleep study. It is information gathering, not a verdict.

Please do not try to diagnose yourself from a video, and do not start or stop any sleep medicine on your own. Chronic sleep trouble is common and often treatable, and a professional can find out what is behind it.

So here is the night in one breath. You drift through light sleep, sink into deep sleep, rise into dreaming, and repeat for four to six cycles, while your brain files memories and may wash away waste.

Sleep is not downtime. It is one of the busiest, most organized things your brain does. Tonight, when you close your eyes, remember that the show is just beginning.

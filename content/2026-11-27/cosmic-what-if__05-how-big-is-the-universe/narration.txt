How big is the universe? To find out, you'll zoom out step by step, until even light, the fastest thing there is, feels painfully slow.

Start with light. It moves about one hundred eighty six thousand miles every second, fast enough to circle the Earth about seven and a half times while you say one Mississippi.

Light from the Moon reaches your eyes in just over a second. Sunlight takes about eight minutes, so you always see the Sun as it was eight minutes ago. Every glance into space is a glance into the past.

Now take Voyager One, launched in nineteen seventy seven and the most distant spacecraft ever built. It is now about sixteen billion miles away, so far that a radio message from Earth takes roughly a full day to reach it.

Almost fifty years of flying, and it has covered about one light day. The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about four and a quarter light years away. At that speed, the trip would take over seventy thousand years.

So what is a light year? It's the distance light travels in a year, almost six trillion miles. If you drove that far at highway speed without ever stopping, the trip would take about ten million years.

In 1990, Voyager One turned its camera back toward home from almost four billion miles away. Earth showed up as a tiny speck floating in a sunbeam, the Pale Blue Dot. And that was still inside our own solar system.

Now zoom out to our galaxy, the Milky Way. It's a spinning disk of more than a hundred billion stars, about one hundred thousand light years across. Our Sun is just one of them, on a spiral arm about halfway out from the center.

And you're moving through it. The solar system races along at about four hundred fifty thousand miles per hour, yet one lap around the galaxy takes about two hundred thirty million years. The Sun has finished only about twenty.

Here's the wild part. Just a century ago, nobody could prove there was anything beyond the Milky Way. Astronomers argued about other galaxies, but they had no evidence. For all anyone knew, our galaxy was the entire universe.

Then, in 1923, Edwin Hubble aimed a giant telescope at a fuzzy cloud called the Andromeda Nebula. Inside it he found a pulsing star whose rhythm reveals its true brightness, and so its distance.

The answer was far beyond the edge of the Milky Way. Andromeda wasn't a cloud at all. It was a whole galaxy of its own, and suddenly the universe was much, much bigger than anyone had imagined.

Andromeda sits about two and a half million light years away. On a dark, clear night you can see it with your own eyes. The light you'd be catching left Andromeda before our species existed.

By 1929, Hubble had found something even stranger. Galaxies are moving away from us, and the farther away a galaxy is, the faster it recedes. The universe isn't standing still. It is expanding.

Here's where it gets dizzying. Starting in two thousand three, astronomers aimed Hubble at a patch of sky that looked empty, about one tenth the width of the full Moon. They gathered light for about a million seconds. That blank spot held roughly ten thousand galaxies.

Those galaxies are also a time machine. The farther you look, the further back in time you see. Some faint smudges in Hubble's deepest images appear as they were less than a billion years after the universe began.

In 2024, the James Webb Space Telescope looked even deeper. It confirmed a galaxy seen as it was about two hundred ninety million years after the Big Bang, when the universe was only about two percent of its current age.

So how many galaxies are out there? Older estimates said one or two hundred billion. A 2016 study using Hubble data found that was at least ten times too low, closer to two trillion. Astronomers are still refining the count.

Multiply that by all their stars, and NASA estimates the universe could hold up to one septillion stars. That's a one followed by twenty four zeros, far more stars than there are grains of sand you could ever count.

Galaxies aren't scattered evenly, either. They gather into groups, clusters and superclusters. In 2014, astronomers mapped ours and named it Laniakea, from Hawaiian words for spacious heaven. It holds about one hundred thousand large galaxies.

Now the biggest number. The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old, so you might expect to see that many light years in every direction. But space has been stretching the whole time. The farthest places whose light we see are now about forty six billion light years away.

That makes the observable universe a sphere about ninety three billion light years across, with you at its center. Not because you're special. Every observer, in every galaxy, sits at the center of their own observable bubble.

So what sits at the edge of that bubble? Not a wall, but a glow: the oldest light we can see, released about three hundred eighty thousand years after the Big Bang. Before that, the universe was a fog light couldn't cross.

And beyond that edge? Almost certainly more universe, whose light hasn't had time to reach us. Some cosmologists think the whole universe is finite but enormous. Others think it goes on forever. Nobody can say yet.

There is one clue. NASA's WMAP mission measured the shape of space and found it flat to within about zero point four percent. A perfectly flat universe could go on forever. Even a gently curved one could dwarf everything we see.

Then came the biggest surprise. In 1998, two teams studying exploding stars found the expansion isn't slowing down. It's speeding up. That discovery won the 2011 Nobel Prize, and what drives it, called dark energy, is still a mystery.

That speed-up has a strange consequence. Distant galaxies are being carried away faster and faster, until their light can no longer reach us. Over unimaginable stretches of time, scientists expect them to slip out of view, one by one.

Here's the twist. Far in the future, astronomers in our corner of space could look out and find nothing beyond home. With no evidence of the rest, they might decide their galaxy is the entire universe, exactly what we believed a century ago.

So how big is the universe, really? At least ninety three billion light years of it is visible, and the rest may never end. And we live early enough to see it, from a tiny blue planet that light crosses in less than a twentieth of a second.

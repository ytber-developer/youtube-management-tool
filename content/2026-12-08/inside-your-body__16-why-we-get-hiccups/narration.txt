You are halfway through a sentence when it happens. Hic. Your body just did something strange, and nobody fully knows why.

Hiccups are one of the oldest mysteries in your body. Almost everyone gets them, doctors can describe them in detail, and yet scientists still argue about what they are actually for. So let us treat this like a detective case.

Every case starts with the scene of the crime. Here it is a muscle called the diaphragm, a thin dome stretched across the bottom of your chest, right under your lungs. It is the main muscle you use to breathe.

Normally your diaphragm moves in a smooth rhythm. It pulls down to draw air in, then relaxes to let air out. You do this all day without noticing, thousands of times, perfectly on beat.

A hiccup breaks that rhythm. Between normal breaths, your diaphragm suddenly jerks, an involuntary spasm. That sudden pull yanks a quick gulp of air in through your mouth and throat.

Then comes the second clue. A split moment later, your vocal cords slam shut and close the opening between them, called the glottis. The incoming air hits that closed door, and you get the sound. Hic.

So the hic is not your stomach and it is not your throat clearing. It is air crashing into your own vocal cords, triggered by a twitch in your breathing muscle. Two parts, perfectly timed, again and again.

Now for the usual suspects, the triggers. Cleveland Clinic lists eating too much or too fast, spicy foods, very hot or very cold food, fizzy drinks, alcohol, smoking, and swallowing extra air.

The NHS adds stress and strong emotions like excitement. And very often, there is no obvious trigger at all. Hiccups can show up while you are sitting perfectly still, doing nothing unusual.

Next, the wiring. Hiccups are a reflex, a loop that runs without your permission. Nerves carry signals from your chest and belly up to your brainstem, and the brainstem fires commands back down to your diaphragm and vocal cords.

That is why you cannot simply decide to stop. The loop lives in the same ancient part of the brain that keeps you breathing while you sleep. Once it starts cycling, it tends to repeat until something resets it.

Here is where the case gets interesting. If hiccups are just a glitch, why do they exist at all? Your body has a reason for coughing, sneezing and swallowing. Hiccups seem to protect nothing. So scientists have proposed some suspects.

Suspect number one is a surprise. You started hiccuping before you were born. Pregnant people can feel the rhythmic jolts, and ultrasound scans show babies hiccuping in the womb, long before they ever breathe air.

In 2003, a team of researchers noticed that the hiccup pattern looks a lot like the way some amphibians, such as tadpoles, pump water over their gills. They breathe in, and then a door snaps shut so water does not flood their lungs.

Their idea is that hiccups might be leftover wiring from ancient ancestors that breathed both water and air. A very old breathing program, still sitting in your brainstem, that occasionally switches on for no good reason.

They found another clue that fits. In their review, hiccups were suppressed when people breathed air with extra carbon dioxide, which is something that also affects gill breathing. A neat match, but not proof.

Suspect number two points at babies again. In 2012, a doctor proposed that hiccups help suckling babies burp. The quick gulp against closed vocal cords may help pull trapped air up from a baby's stomach.

That would let a baby fit in more milk during a feed, and it might explain why hiccups are so common in infants. Cleveland Clinic notes that hiccups are normal in babies, likely linked to gas in the stomach.

Suspect number three is the newest. In 2019, scientists at University College London recorded the brain waves of newborn babies while they hiccuped. Each hiccup set off a strong burst of activity in the baby's brain.

Their idea is that every hic is a kind of training signal. It may help a newborn's brain learn where its breathing muscles are and how they feel, which could help later control of breathing. It is a hypothesis, not a settled fact.

So which suspect is guilty? The honest answer is that the case is still open. The ideas are not even mutually exclusive. Adult hiccups might simply be an old infant reflex that never fully switched off.

Now, what about stopping them? Everyone has a favorite cure: holding your breath, sipping ice water, swallowing a spoonful of sugar, biting a lemon, or breathing into a paper bag. Never use a plastic bag.

Here is the catch. Both Cleveland Clinic and the NHS say these tricks have little evidence and do not work for everyone. They are mostly harmless, though, and some may interrupt the reflex loop for a moment.

The good news is that most hiccups stop on their own within a few minutes. To avoid them, eat more slowly, go easy on fizzy drinks, and skip very hot food right after something very cold.

But there is one rule worth remembering. If hiccups last longer than two days, contact a doctor. Hiccups that go on that long are called persistent, and sometimes they point to another health problem that needs attention.

Long bouts can make it hard to eat, sleep or talk. And if hiccups ever come with signs of a stroke, like a drooping face or sudden weakness, call emergency services right away. That is rare, but important.

So, case summary. A hiccup is a twitch of your diaphragm and a slam of your vocal cords. It runs on an ancient reflex loop, and it may be a relic of water breathing, a baby's burp, or brain training.

The next time you hiccup, you are not just being interrupted. You might be hearing the echo of an old program your body has carried since before you were born. Hic.

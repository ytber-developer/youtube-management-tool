What if you fell into the Sun? What would you see, and how far would you get?

Here is the first surprise. Falling into the Sun is incredibly hard. The Sun is pulling on you right now, but you are not falling in, because you are moving sideways, fast.

Earth, and everything on it, races around the Sun at about sixty-seven thousand miles per hour. That speed points almost entirely sideways. So you keep falling toward the Sun and keep missing it. That is what an orbit is.

To hit the Sun, you have to cancel almost all of that sideways speed. NASA says reaching the Sun takes about fifty-five times more energy than going to Mars.

Even NASA's Parker Solar Probe, built to fly closer to the Sun than anything before it, needed a powerful rocket plus seven flybys of Venus to shed enough sideways motion.

And Parker was not even trying to hit the Sun, just to swoop past it. That alone meant dropping about fifty-three thousand miles per hour of sideways speed. Hitting it dead center would mean losing nearly all sixty-seven thousand.

So let's cheat. Imagine a giant hand grabs you, out in space where Earth orbits, and stops you dead. No sideways speed at all. Then it lets go.

How long until you reach the Sun? Astronomers can work it out with Kepler's laws. The answer is about sixty-five days. You would fall for more than two months.

At first, it does not feel like falling at all. You are weightless, drifting, and the Sun looks the same size it always does. For weeks, you barely seem to move.

Behind you, Earth shrinks to a blue dot. You started about ninety-three million miles from the Sun. That is so far that sunlight, the fastest thing there is, needs a little over eight minutes to cross it.

But gravity grows stronger as you get closer, and you pick up speed every second. Most of your speed comes right at the end. The Sun swells in front of you, bigger every day.

And it is big. About one point three million Earths could fit inside it. It holds ninety-nine point eight percent of all the mass in the solar system. Everything else, every planet, is a rounding error.

Line up Earths side by side, and it would take about a hundred of them to stretch across the Sun's face. From up close, it is not a disk in the sky anymore. It is a glowing wall.

Your real problem, long before you arrive, is sunlight. With no air and no shade, the light gets more intense the closer you fall. Your suit would be roasting, and then failing, well before the final day.

But this is a thought experiment, so let's give you an imaginary suit that cannot melt, and keep falling. Up ahead is a marker: the closest any spacecraft has ever come to the Sun.

On Christmas Eve, 2024, Parker Solar Probe swept just three point eight million miles above the Sun's surface, moving at four hundred thirty thousand miles per hour. That made it the fastest object people have ever built.

Parker survives because of a heat shield about four and a half inches thick. Its sunny side heats to about two thousand five hundred degrees Fahrenheit, while the spacecraft behind it sits at around eighty-five degrees.

Here is something strange. At that distance, Parker is flying through the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, where the gas is more than a million degrees Fahrenheit. That is much hotter than the Sun's visible surface.

NASA says the corona can reach about three and a half million degrees Fahrenheit. Why the Sun's outer atmosphere is so much hotter than its surface is one of the big puzzles Parker was sent to help solve.

So why doesn't Parker melt? Because temperature and heat are not the same thing. Temperature measures how fast particles move. Heat is how much energy they actually deliver to you.

The corona is incredibly thin. Its particles are blazing fast, but there are so few of them that they hit the shield rarely. It is like putting your hand in a hot oven versus boiling water.

You can wave your hand through hot oven air for a moment. Plunge it into boiling water at a lower temperature, and you get burned, because far more particles touch you.

For you, falling through the corona, the danger is not the million-degree gas. It is the blinding wall of light ahead. And by now, the Sun fills your entire view.

Then you reach the photosphere, the part of the Sun we see from Earth. It looks like a surface. It isn't one. The Sun is a ball of glowing plasma, with nothing solid to land on.

The photosphere is about ten thousand degrees Fahrenheit. And you would hit it at more than one point three million miles per hour, close to the Sun's escape speed of about six hundred eighteen kilometers per second.

At that speed and temperature, even your magic suit has given up. In reality, you would vaporize into a puff of hot gas and simply become part of the Sun. But let's imagine your ghost keeps going down.

Below the surface is the convection zone. Here, the Sun boils like a pot of soup. Giant bubbles of hot plasma rise, cool, and sink again, carrying energy outward.

The Sun's gravity at the surface is about twenty-eight times stronger than Earth's. If you could stand there, you would weigh about twenty-eight times what you weigh now. Deeper down, the pressure crushes everything.

Under the convection zone lies the radiative zone. Here, energy creeps out as light, absorbed and re-emitted again and again, bouncing in a random path through incredibly dense plasma.

This is so slow that NASA says energy takes about one hundred seventy thousand years to travel from the core to the top of the convection zone. The energy in the sunlight warming your face today began that journey long before the first cities.

And then, finally, the core. It is about twenty-seven million degrees Fahrenheit. The pressure is so great that hydrogen nuclei are squeezed together into helium. This is nuclear fusion.

The core is enormous, about eighty-six thousand miles thick. You could line up ten Earths across it and still have room to spare. It is the furnace that powers everything you have ever seen in daylight.

Fusion turns a little mass into a huge amount of energy. NASA says the Sun converts roughly four million tons of its own material into heat and light every single second.

Now think about what you just did to the Sun. You added a person's worth of mass. Meanwhile, it loses about four million tons every second. Your whole body would not even register.

And you would not be the first visitor to fall in. Comets dive close to the Sun all the time. Many of these sungrazers break apart and vaporize in its glare.

The ESA and NASA spacecraft SOHO, which watches the Sun, has discovered more than five thousand comets, many of them spotted by volunteers on home computers. In March 2024, a student found number five thousand.

So the Sun swallows things all the time, and never flinches. It has been shining for billions of years, and it will keep shining long after your fall.

So, what if you fell into the Sun? First, you would need to stop moving sideways, which is wildly hard. Then you would fall for sixty-five days, cook in the light, and vanish without the Sun even noticing.

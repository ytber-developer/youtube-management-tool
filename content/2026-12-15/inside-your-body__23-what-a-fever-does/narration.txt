You feel awful, your forehead is burning, and you are shivering under three blankets. Your body is not broken. It is doing this on purpose.

Most of us think of a fever as part of the illness itself. But a fever is something your body does deliberately, a carefully controlled response. Let's walk through it step by step, like a battle plan.

Step zero is your normal setting. Body temperature averages about ninety eight point six degrees Fahrenheit, or thirty seven Celsius. It naturally drifts by around half a degree Celsius during the day, and is usually highest in the evening.

That temperature is managed by a thermostat in your brain, a region called the hypothalamus. It compares your actual temperature to a target, called the set point, and makes adjustments to stay close to it.

Step one is the invasion. Say bacteria or a virus slips into your body. Immune cells such as macrophages and dendritic cells are on patrol, and they recognize telltale molecules from the invaders.

Step two is the alarm. Those immune cells release chemical messengers, including cytokines called interleukin one, interleukin six, and tumor necrosis factor. These signals act throughout the body, and some reach the brain.

Step three is the reset. In the brain, a messenger called prostaglandin E two acts on temperature control neurons in the hypothalamus. The result is that the set point is raised to a higher number.

This is the key idea. Your temperature is not out of control. Your brain has deliberately chosen a higher target, and now your whole body is going to work hard to reach it.

Here is the strange part. Your body is still at its old temperature, but your brain now treats that as too cold. So you feel chilly, even when the room around you is warm.

Step four is turning up the heat. Your nervous system releases norepinephrine, which fires up heat production in brown fat and narrows blood vessels in your hands and feet to keep heat from escaping.

That is why your fingers and toes can feel cold while your fever is climbing. Your body is limiting heat loss through your skin to keep warmth deep inside, around your core.

Then come the shivers. Signals tell your muscles to contract rapidly, turning stored chemical energy into heat. Those chattering teeth are a tiny furnace that your brain has switched on.

You also change your behavior. You want blankets, a warm bed, maybe a hot drink. Those cravings help you hold onto heat. In a way, you are acting like a lizard basking on a warm rock.

That comparison is more than a joke. Cold blooded animals like reptiles, fish, and even insects cannot make much of their own heat. When they are infected, they move to warmer places to raise their temperature.

In classic experiments from the nineteen seventies, infected desert iguanas that were kept from warming themselves by about two degrees Celsius were far less likely to survive. Their own fever was protecting them.

Even honeybees do a version of this. During an infection, worker bees can raise the temperature of the hive itself through increased physical activity. A whole colony runs a fever together.

In fact, a review in Nature Reviews Immunology notes that fever has been kept by evolution in warm blooded and cold blooded animals for more than six hundred million years. A trait that old usually earns its keep.

And fever is expensive. The same review estimates that raising body temperature by just one degree Celsius takes roughly ten to twelve and a half percent more metabolic energy.

That energy bill is one reason a fever can leave you feeling drained and sleepy. Your body is spending a lot of fuel on making heat and on fighting the infection.

Step five is the payoff. What does all that heat buy you? First, it can make life harder for some germs. In lab studies, fever range temperatures slowed the copying of poliovirus in mammalian cells more than two hundred fold.

Fever temperatures can also make certain bacteria easier for the natural defenses in your blood to destroy. The heat does not just make you uncomfortable. It can put pressure on the invaders.

Second, heat seems to boost your immune system. Researchers describe fever helping immune cells called lymphocytes travel to your lymph nodes and other lymphoid organs, the staging grounds where your defense is organized.

And one of the messengers that triggered your fever turns out to have a second job. Interleukin six also helps guide those lymphocytes to where they need to go. One signal, two roles.

Step six is the break. As the infection comes under control, the alarm signals fade and the set point drops back toward normal. Suddenly your body is warmer than its new target.

So everything runs in reverse. Blood vessels near your skin open up to release heat, and you start to sweat. People say the fever broke, and that is exactly what it feels like.

Fever is not perfect, though. The same review points out that fever is not always beneficial, and that some of its effects can be harmful. That is one reason doctors watch how high a fever goes.

Doctors sort fevers roughly by height. One medical reference calls about one hundred point six to one hundred two point two degrees Fahrenheit moderate, and above one hundred two point four high.

There is also a different problem called hyperthermia, where body temperature rises beyond what the hypothalamus is controlling. That is not a fever. It is the thermostat itself being overwhelmed.

So when should you call a doctor? MedlinePlus advises contacting a health care provider if an adult's fever stays at or keeps rising above one hundred three degrees Fahrenheit, or lasts longer than two to three days.

For a baby three months old or younger, a rectal temperature of one hundred point four or higher means calling a provider right away. Get emergency help for trouble breathing, confusion, a stiff neck, or a seizure.

How to manage a fever is a decision for you and your health care provider. But science is clear on one thing. MedlinePlus calls fever an important part of the body's defense against infection.

So next time you are shivering under those blankets, remember what is happening. Your brain turned up the thermostat on purpose, your muscles are making heat, and your immune system is fighting harder because of it.

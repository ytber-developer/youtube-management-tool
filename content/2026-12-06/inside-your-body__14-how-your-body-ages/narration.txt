Your brain's processing speed likely peaked around your early twenties. But one of your mental abilities may keep improving into your late sixties.

Aging is something every human body does, yet most of us picture it as a single clock ticking down. The real picture is far more interesting. Your body is full of different clocks, running at different speeds, and a few of them run in your favor.

To understand those clocks, you have to start small. Very small. Aging begins inside your cells, with a handful of processes scientists call the hallmarks of aging, the core machinery behind how bodies change over time.

The first is damage. By one estimate, the DNA in each of your cells is damaged up to a million times a day. Your cells repair almost all of it. But repair is not perfect, and over a lifetime, small errors slowly pile up.

The second is a kind of countdown. At the ends of your chromosomes sit protective caps called telomeres, repeated stretches of DNA that protect the ends of your chromosomes. Telomeres tend to get shorter with age, in people and in other animals like mice.

When a telomere gets critically short, the cell notices and permanently switches off its ability to divide. Think of the plastic tip on a shoelace. Once it wears away, the lace starts to fray, so the cell stops dividing altogether.

That leads to the third hallmark, and it is a strange one. Some of those cells that stop dividing do not die. They linger. Scientists call them senescent cells, and they release harmful molecules into the tissue around them.

Add a few more processes. Your stem cells, which rebuild tissue, gradually lose some of their ability to regenerate. And your mitochondria, the power plants in your cells, produce energy along with reactive byproducts. Scientists describe the ideal level of those byproducts as a Goldilocks zone: not too much, and not too little.

None of this happens overnight, and none of it happens at the same pace in every person. But over decades, these tiny cellular shifts add up to changes you can see and feel. So follow them through a lifetime, starting earlier than you might think.

Some of your abilities peak remarkably early. One large study of more than forty eight thousand volunteers, published in two thousand fifteen, found that raw processing speed peaks in the late teens and early twenties, and then begins to decline relatively quickly.

Working memory, your ability to hold and juggle information in your head, peaks a little later, in the late twenties to early thirties. After that, it declines slowly.

Your muscles start changing around then, too. For most people, muscle mass begins a gradual decline of about three to five percent each decade, starting around age thirty. Muscle fibers shrink, and some lost muscle tissue may be replaced with tougher, fibrous tissue.

Scientists call this age-related muscle loss sarcopenia. Aging itself plays a role, along with hormonal changes and fewer muscle-building proteins. Physical inactivity and some diseases can speed it up.

Then, somewhere in your forties, you may notice something odd. Your arms suddenly seem too short. You catch yourself holding your phone farther away to read it. There is a simple reason for this, and almost no one escapes it.

It is called presbyopia, and it usually begins after age forty five. Inside your eye, the lens has to change shape to focus on things up close. With age, that lens becomes harder and less flexible, so it can no longer focus nearby light correctly. Eventually, everyone gets it.

But here is the first piece of good news. In that same large study, the ability to read other people's emotions peaked in the forties to around age fifty, and did not start to decline significantly until after sixty.

Your bones are changing through these decades too. People lose bone mass and density as they age, especially women after menopause, as minerals like calcium are lost from the skeleton.

And here is a fun connection. Remember how astronauts grow taller in space because their spines relax? With age, the opposite happens. The discs between your vertebrae gradually lose fluid and become thinner, your trunk shortens, and overall height decreases. Even the arches of your feet can flatten a little, shaving off a bit more.

Joints change as well. They can stiffen and become less flexible, and the cartilage that cushions them may start to wear away, especially in the hips and knees. That is part of why getting up from the floor can feel different at sixty than it did at twenty.

So far, you have seen the changes you can feel. But some of the most important ones are invisible, happening inside the organ that has been beating for you since before you were born.

With age, your heart's natural pacemaker, the sinoatrial node, loses some of its cells, which can slightly slow your heart rate. The heart wall thickens, and the heart may fill with blood more slowly.

Your largest artery, the aorta, becomes thicker, stiffer and less flexible. That raises blood pressure and makes your heart work harder. And the pressure sensors in your blood vessels become less sensitive, which is one reason some older adults feel dizzy when they stand up quickly.

Your skin tells the story on the outside. The outer layer thins, the connective tissue that gives skin strength and stretch weakens, and the cushioning fat underneath shrinks. Sweat and oil glands produce less, so skin gets drier.

Those skin changes affect more than appearance. With less fat beneath the skin, there is less padding and insulation, so it can be harder to stay warm. And with sweat glands producing less sweat, it becomes easier to overheat. Your built-in thermostat loses some of its range.

But here is a surprise. According to MedlinePlus, from the National Library of Medicine, the greatest single factor in how skin ages is not time at all. It is sun exposure. Compare skin that rarely sees the sun with skin that does, and the difference can be striking.

Your ears change too. About one in three Americans between sixty five and seventy four has hearing loss, and nearly half of those older than seventy five have difficulty hearing. Changes in the inner ear are common with age, and years of noise exposure add up. Conditions like high blood pressure and diabetes, and some medicines, can also affect hearing.

Which brings you to the organ people worry about most as they get older. What happens to the brain, the place where your memories, your personality and your sense of self all live?

The honest answer is that it does change. With age, the brain loses some nerve cells and shrinks slightly, and nerve signals may travel a little more slowly. Some slowing of thought and memory is a normal part of aging, and it varies widely from person to person.

But it is important to know what is not normal. Dementia and severe memory loss are not a normal part of aging. They are linked to diseases, such as Alzheimer's disease. Sudden confusion is not normal either, and it is often caused by other illnesses.

Now for the clock that runs the other way. Remember the ability that may keep improving into your late sixties? In the Harvard study, vocabulary, a measure of accumulated knowledge, had no early peak at all. Scores kept rising until about sixty five to seventy. Knowledge, it seems, keeps compounding like interest.

Older data had suggested vocabulary peaked much earlier. But as the researchers looked at survey data across the decades, the age of peak vocabulary kept moving later, from the early forties in the nineteen seventies and eighties to about sixty five in more recent years.

And there may be an even bigger surprise. At Stanford University, researchers followed one hundred eighty four people, aged eighteen to ninety four, for ten years. At the start, after five years and after ten years, participants carried pagers for a week and rated their emotions five times a day.

On average, as people got older, their emotional lives became more positive. The researchers describe it as a shift over time rather than proof of cause and effect. But it pushes against a common assumption, that getting older means getting gloomier.

So what really happens to your body as you age? Your cells collect damage, your telomeres shorten, and some cells stop dividing and linger. Muscles slowly shrink, bones thin, lenses stiffen, arteries harden, and hearing fades.

But at the same time, your knowledge keeps building, your ability to read other people stays strong for decades, and your emotional life may grow richer. At almost every age, you are getting worse at some things, better at others, and holding steady on the rest.

Aging is not a single clock running down. It is a whole orchestra of clocks, each keeping its own time. And some of the best music, it turns out, comes later in the song.

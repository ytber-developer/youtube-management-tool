Look up at Orion on a winter night. That reddish star on his shoulder is going to explode. Here's what tonight would look like.

First, two promises. This is completely safe for Earth, and you'll see exactly why. And tonight is a thought experiment. Astronomers think Betelgeuse has roughly a hundred thousand years left. It could go in your lifetime, but the odds are it won't.

To find it, look for Orion's belt, three bright stars in a neat row. In the Northern Hemisphere, Betelgeuse sits above and to the left, glowing a distinct orange red. NASA calls it the tenth brightest star in our night sky, and the closest red supergiant to Earth.

Betelgeuse is a red supergiant, one of the biggest stars you can see with your bare eyes. One study puts it at about seven hundred fifty times the width of the Sun. Drop it where our Sun is, and its surface would swallow Mercury, Venus, Earth, and Mars, reaching beyond the asteroid belt.

Some estimates make it even bigger, reaching all the way out toward the orbit of Jupiter. Either way, it weighs in at roughly sixteen to nineteen times the mass of the Sun, and it is near the end of a life measured in just millions of years.

Stars that heavy burn through their fuel fast. They fuse hydrogen, then helium, then heavier and heavier elements, layer by layer, like an onion. The final layer at the center is iron. And iron is a dead end, because fusing it doesn't release energy. It costs energy.

So imagine the moment comes. The iron core, a bit heavier than our entire Sun, can no longer hold itself up. In about a second, it collapses. A ball the size of Earth is crushed into a sphere roughly the size of a city, made almost entirely of neutrons.

The collapse bounces, and a shock wave blasts outward through the star. It will take hours to reach the surface. But something escapes immediately: an enormous burst of ghostly particles called neutrinos. And here's the strange part. On Earth, they arrive first.

Here's how lopsided that is. Almost all of the energy of the collapse leaves as neutrinos, not light. In a few seconds, physicists calculate, they carry away roughly a hundred times more energy than our Sun will give off over its entire ten-billion-year life.

Neutrinos pass through almost anything. Trillions upon trillions of them would stream through your body tonight, and you would feel nothing. But giant detectors buried underground would light up. Physicists expect the future DUNE experiment, deep under South Dakota, could catch around a million of them.

Compare that to Supernova nineteen eighty-seven A, the closest one in modern times, in a small neighbor galaxy about two hundred forty times farther away than Betelgeuse. Detectors around the world caught only about two dozen neutrinos from it.

Those detectors could give astronomers up to about twelve hours of warning before the light arrives. An automated network exists for exactly this. Around the world, astronomers' phones would buzz with alerts, and telescopes would swing toward Orion's shoulder, waiting.

Then the shock wave breaks through the surface, and the light arrives. Simulations by astronomers at UC Santa Barbara suggest Betelgeuse would blaze about as bright as a half Moon for more than three months. But all of that light would come from a single, blinding point.

Picture it. A star so bright it casts shadows at night. Orion, the hunter, carrying a dazzling spark on his shoulder. And during the day, you could still see it in a clear blue sky, for roughly a year by one estimate.

Then it would fade, slowly, over years. For several years it would remain visible to the naked eye at night. And when it finally dims away, Orion would be missing a shoulder. The most famous constellation in the sky would be changed for every human who ever lived after you.

Now, the question everyone asks. Is this dangerous? According to NASA, a supernova would have to explode less than about fifty light-years away to damage Earth's ozone layer. Betelgeuse is around ten times farther than that. There are no nearby stars capable of exploding that close.

NASA estimates that one or two supernovas explode somewhere in our galaxy every century. Yet the last one anyone spotted in the Milky Way with the naked eye was more than four hundred years ago. Astronomers think many others were simply hidden behind thick clouds of galactic dust.

So no radiation storm, no extinction, nothing to hide from. Just the most spectacular light show in recorded history. And there's a deeper twist hiding in that word, tonight. Because whatever you see tonight has already happened.

Betelgeuse is roughly five hundred to seven hundred light-years away, depending on which measurement you trust. So the light you see from it tonight left the star centuries ago, somewhere around the late Middle Ages. If you see it explode tonight, it really exploded long before anyone had ever looked through a telescope.

It's also possible that Betelgeuse has already exploded, and the light just hasn't reached us yet. Astronomers don't think that's likely, because the star doesn't seem to be in its final stage. But we genuinely can't rule it out.

Which brings us to the scare of twenty nineteen. That winter, Betelgeuse suddenly dimmed so much that people could see the difference with their bare eyes. Headlines asked whether it was about to blow. It wasn't. But what had happened turned out to be almost as dramatic.

Using the Hubble Space Telescope and other observatories, astronomers found that Betelgeuse had blown off a huge piece of its visible surface. The material drifted away, cooled, and formed a cloud of dust that blocked part of the star's light. Nothing like it had ever been seen on a normal star.

Our Sun throws off material too, but its eruptions are many orders of magnitude weaker. The blast even disrupted a steady pulse of brightening and dimming, about every four hundred days, that astronomers had tracked for more than two hundred years.

So, was that a sign of the end? A twenty twenty study modeling Betelgeuse's pulsations concluded that it is still in an early stage of burning helium in its core, likely more than a hundred thousand years before it explodes. The same study suggested it's smaller and closer than earlier thought.

And Betelgeuse had one more secret. In twenty twenty-five, NASA scientists using a camera on the Gemini North telescope in Hawaii found a faint companion star, orbiting very close to its outer edge. It's been nicknamed Siwarha, which means her bracelet.

Astronomers will get their best look at it around November twenty twenty-seven, when it swings out to its widest point. That little star probably has a grim future, because one day the giant beside it will explode.

When it does, the expanding debris will glow for thousands of years. Look at the Crab Nebula, the wreckage of a star whose explosion was recorded by astronomers in the year one thousand fifty-four. It's still expanding, with a spinning neutron star at its heart.

Betelgeuse will probably leave behind a neutron star too, though some astronomers think it could form a black hole. Its debris will carry oxygen, silicon, calcium, and many other elements it spent its whole life building, out into the galaxy.

And that's the real payoff. The oxygen in the air you're breathing right now, and many of the atoms in your body, were made inside massive stars like Betelgeuse and scattered when they died. Supernovas aren't only destroyers. They're how the universe makes things like you.

So next time you see Orion, find that red shoulder and remember what you're looking at: a giant star near the end of its life, glowing with light from centuries ago. One night, maybe not for thousands of years, it will turn into the brightest star anyone has ever seen.

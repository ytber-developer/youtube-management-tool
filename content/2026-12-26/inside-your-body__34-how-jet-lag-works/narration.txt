You land in Paris at breakfast time. Your body insists it is the middle of the night. Who is right, your clock or the world?

Say you board an overnight flight from New York. Paris is six hours ahead. You step off the plane at eight in the morning local time, but back home, and inside your body, it is two in the morning.

That gap is jet lag. The CDC describes it as a sleep disorder caused by rapid travel across time zones. Your internal clock falls out of step with the clock on the wall.

To see why it feels so rough, meet your body clock. Nearly everything about you, from sleep and alertness to hormones, body temperature, and digestion, rises and falls on a roughly twenty-four hour cycle.

Scientists call these circadian rhythms, from Latin words meaning about a day. They are not just habits. They are built into you, and they keep ticking even when you have no clock or window to look at.

Your master clock sits deep in your brain, in a tiny cluster called the suprachiasmatic nucleus. It holds about twenty thousand nerve cells, and each one of them can keep time on its own.

Those twenty thousand tiny clocks talk to each other and stay in sync. Together, they are accurate to within a few minutes across an entire day. Not bad for a clump of cells you have never even seen.

Its long name simply describes where it sits: right above the spot where the nerves from your two eyes cross. That makes it perfectly placed to hear about one thing above all else, light.

But here is the twist. Your master clock does not run on exactly twenty-four hours. In a well-known Harvard study, it averaged about twenty-four hours and eleven minutes. Remember that number. It matters later.

So every day, your clock needs a small nudge to stay matched to the sun. The main thing that nudges it is light, and it gets that light information through your eyes.

Your eyes contain special light-sensing cells that are not mainly for seeing. They use a pigment called melanopsin to gauge how bright your surroundings are, then send that signal straight to your master clock.

When evening comes and the light fades, your master clock signals your brain to make more melatonin, a hormone that helps bring on sleep. By morning, with daylight back, that sleepy signal fades.

And your master clock is not alone. Nearly every tissue and organ in your body has its own rhythm. One of your master clock's main jobs is to keep all of those body clocks in sync.

Think of it as an orchestra. The master clock is the conductor, and your organs are the musicians. On a normal day, they all play together, so you feel sleepy at night and hungry at mealtimes.

Now, back to Paris. It is eight in the morning and the sun is up, but your conductor is still playing the two in the morning part. Your body clock is set for deep sleep, not sightseeing.

So you feel foggy and heavy. You order a croissant at a café, but your stomach does not seem sure it is breakfast time at all. To your gut, it is still the middle of the night.

That is jet lag in action. The CDC lists trouble sleeping, daytime sleepiness, slower thinking, a general unwell feeling, and stomach and digestion problems, often showing up within a day or two of travel.

It takes crossing at least two time zones to cause true jet lag, and the more zones you cross, the worse it tends to be. Six time zones, like New York to Paris, is a big jump.

That night, you crawl into bed at eleven. But your body thinks it is only five in the evening. You lie there, staring at the ceiling, wide awake while the whole city sleeps around you.

The next morning, the alarm rings at seven, and your body thinks it is one in the morning. That is the classic eastbound trap. Hard to fall asleep at night, and hard to drag yourself up in the morning.

Here is the good news. Your clock does adjust, just not all at once. According to the CDC, after flying east, people adapt by about one hour per day, on average.

So for a six hour jump to Paris, it can take close to a week before your body is fully running on local time. That can be most of a short vacation spent feeling a little off.

Now, the flight home. You fly west, back to New York. It is the same six hours, just in the other direction. And yet most people find this trip noticeably easier. Why would that be?

Flying west, people adapt faster, about an hour and a half per day on average. So the same six hours might take about four days instead of six. The reason comes back to that odd number.

Remember, your clock naturally runs a little longer than twenty-four hours. So it tends to drift later. The CDC notes that is why most people find it easier to push their bedtime later than earlier.

Flying west makes your day longer. You simply stay up later, which goes along with your clock's natural drift. Flying east makes your day shorter, so you have to force your clock earlier, against the grain.

So can you speed things up? Light is your most powerful tool, but timing is everything. Light at the wrong time of day can actually push your clock in the wrong direction.

After flying east, get bright light in the morning and avoid bright light in the evening, because morning light pulls your clock earlier. After flying west, it is the reverse. Avoid bright early morning light.

You can also get a head start. Before an eastbound trip, the CDC suggests shifting your sleep about one hour earlier each day, for two or three days before you leave.

Your flight choice matters too. Losing sleep on the plane can make jet lag worse, and the CDC notes that picking a flight that lands at a good time of day for your new schedule can help.

On the plane, drink water and go easy on alcohol and caffeine. Alcohol may help you doze off, but it tends to break up your sleep, and both can leave you dehydrated, which can make jet lag worse.

Caffeine can help you stay alert during daylight at your destination. But it lingers. It takes about five hours for your body to clear half of it, so skip it in the hours before bedtime.

What about melatonin pills? Timing matters for them too, and supplements are not regulated by the FDA, so what is in the bottle may not match the label. Ask a doctor or pharmacist before trying them, or any sleep medicine.

For most people, jet lag fades on its own as the body clock catches up. But if sleep problems hang on long after you are home, or keep coming back, it is worth talking to a doctor.

So jet lag is not just being tired. It is twenty thousand tiny clocks in your brain, and the clocks in nearly every organ, suddenly playing a different song than the sun.

But your body can change its tempo, one hour at a time. Give it light at the right moments, a little patience, and a few days, and the whole orchestra will find the beat again.

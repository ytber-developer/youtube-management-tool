What if you could watch the first second of the universe? Nothing you know about space prepares you for it.

First, a warning. The Big Bang was not an explosion in empty space, and nobody could stand outside and watch. So think of this as a movie built from physics, not a camera recording.

The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old. Scientists reconstruct its first moments from particle experiments, from the elements we see, and from ancient light that still fills the sky.

Press play. At the very start, we honestly do not know. The tiniest fraction of a second is the least certain part of the story, and physics as we know it may break down.

The best-supported idea is cosmic inflation, a brief burst of extremely fast expansion. NASA says scientists don't know what caused it. But it would explain why the universe looks so smooth and even.

By some estimates, inflation lasted only a trillionth of a trillionth of a trillionth of a second or so, yet stretched space by roughly a hundred trillion trillion times. Timings vary between models.

When inflation ended, its energy turned into a hot mix of particles and light. That is the moment we usually call the Big Bang. Everything the universe would ever be was now a blazing, dense fog.

Roughly a trillionth of a second later, by the usual estimates, the universe cooled enough for a big change. Forces that had acted as one split apart, and particles like the W and Z gained mass.

The temperature was about a thousand trillion degrees. Nothing like an atom, a star or a planet existed yet, and no one knows for sure what the universe looked like even earlier. If you were somehow there, you would not be a person. You would be part of the soup.

Next came the quark epoch. Space was filled with quarks and gluons, the building blocks of protons and neutrons, zipping around too hot and too energetic to stick together. Physicists call this a quark-gluon plasma.

We have even made tiny drops of it. Heavy ions smashed together at particle colliders briefly recreate similar conditions, which helps scientists test this part of the timeline on Earth.

At around ten microseconds, this plasma had cooled to about a trillion degrees, and quarks finally locked together. Protons and neutrons were born, along with other heavy particles.

Their opposites, antimatter, were made too. Matter and antimatter wiped each other out in flashes of light. For reasons still unknown, a tiny extra bit of matter survived. That leftover is us.

Fun fact: every galaxy, star and person is made from a leftover too small to imagine. Scientists are still hunting for why matter came out ahead, and that question is far from settled.

By about one second after the beginning, the temperature had dropped to roughly ten billion degrees. That is still unimaginably hot, but enormously cooler than before.

At this point, ghostly neutrinos stopped bumping into everything and flew free. They still fill the universe today, a faint cosmic neutrino background. They barely interact with anything, so they are famously hard to catch.

So that is the first second: a quick, violent blur. You would have blinked and missed all of it, and in truth you could not have watched anyway. The universe was far too dense for light to travel far.

Now fast forward a little. Over the next several minutes, protons and neutrons began fusing together. This is Big Bang nucleosynthesis, and it ran for only about twenty minutes before the universe grew too cool and thin.

Neutrons are unstable by themselves, so many had decayed. By the time fusion started, there was roughly one neutron for every seven protons. That ratio decided what the universe would be made of.

First, some protons and neutrons made deuterium, a heavy form of hydrogen. Then most of the remaining neutrons ended up in helium. The result was about three quarters hydrogen and one quarter helium by mass.

There were only tiny traces of heavier things, like lithium. Anything bigger would have to wait for stars. Carbon, oxygen and iron in your body were made later, inside stellar furnaces and explosions.

Here is a loose thread. Models predict more lithium than we measure in old stars, by a factor of about two to four. That puzzle is called the lithium problem, and scientists still debate the answer.

And then, for a very long time, nothing much happened to our movie. The universe was a glowing, opaque fog of free electrons and nuclei. Light kept bouncing around, trapped, like headlights in thick mist.

The fog lasted for hundreds of thousands of years. Then, about three hundred eighty thousand years after the start, the universe cooled to a few thousand degrees and atoms could finally form.

Electrons settled onto nuclei, the fog cleared, and light was suddenly free to travel across space. NASA calls what we see from that moment the oldest light we can observe in the universe.

That light is still all around you. As space stretched, it cooled from thousands of degrees to about two point seven degrees above absolute zero. It now arrives as faint microwaves, not visible glow.

Two Bell Labs engineers, Arno Penzias and Robert Wilson, found it by accident in 1964, as an unexplained hiss in their antenna. They shared the Nobel Prize in physics for the discovery in 1978.

The CMB is almost perfectly even, hotter and cooler spots differing by only about one part in twenty-five thousand. Those tiny ripples were seeds. Gravity later pulled them into stars and galaxies.

Satellites like COBE and Planck mapped these ripples. Planck's data gave the age of the universe as about thirteen point eight billion years, with an uncertainty of only a few tens of millions.

So what could you actually see if you watched the first second? Nothing, really. There was no outside, no darkness to see against, and no light that travelled freely. It would be a wall of glowing plasma.

But the clues are all still here: in hydrogen and helium, in neutrinos, in the microwave glow, in particle colliders. Together they give us a timeline that has held up to strong tests. Parts remain open.

Notice what stays uncertain: what drove inflation, why matter won, and what came before. Cosmologists use phrases like probably and most likely for good reason. The first second is still being written.

So, what if you could watch the first second? You would see a universe going from unimaginably hot and tiny to a sea of particles ready to fuse. The rest is thirteen billion years of cooling and building.

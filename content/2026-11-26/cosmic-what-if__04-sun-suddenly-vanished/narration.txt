Right now, the Sun disappears. No explosion, no warning. Strangely, for the next eight minutes, you would have no idea.

That's because sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to cross the ninety three million miles to Earth. The light warming your face right now left the Sun more than eight minutes ago. For a little while, you'd still be basking in the glow of a star that's gone.

Here's the part that surprises people. Earth would keep orbiting that empty spot for the same eight minutes, because changes in gravity also travel at the speed of light. In twenty seventeen, gravitational waves and light from colliding neutron stars, one hundred thirty million light years away, arrived within about two seconds of each other.

Then, at eight minutes and twenty seconds, the sky goes black. The daytime side of Earth plunges into night. The Moon vanishes too, since moonlight is just reflected sunlight. The bright planets fade away. Only the distant stars remain.

At the same moment, Earth is set free. Our planet has been racing around the Sun at about sixty seven thousand miles per hour. With nothing pulling it inward, it flies off in a straight line toward interstellar space, taking the Moon along for the ride.

Now the cold. Earth doesn't freeze instantly, because land, air, and especially oceans store a lot of heat. But Caltech planetary scientist David Stevenson estimates that within about a week, the surface would cool below the freezing point of salt water, and ice would start spreading across the seas.

Photosynthesis stops the instant the light does. Most plants would die within a few weeks. Some big trees, with their stored sugars, might hang on for decades. But the food chain that feeds you would collapse long before that.

After about a year, the surface would sit around minus one hundred degrees Fahrenheit. Over millions of years, it would keep cooling toward about minus four hundred. Long before that, the air itself would freeze out of the sky as solid oxygen and nitrogen.

And yet, Earth would not be completely dead. The ice forming on the oceans would act like a lid, insulating the water below for a very long time. And deep underground, microbes that never needed sunlight could carry on, warmed by heat from Earth's interior.

Could people survive? A few, possibly. Iceland already heats close to nine out of ten homes with geothermal energy. Scientists suggest that with volcanic heat and habitats dug deep underground, small groups could hold on for a long time.

The good news: the Sun can't actually vanish. Mass doesn't just disappear. Our star is about four point six billion years old, and when it finally changes, billions of years from now, it will swell, not blink out. So enjoy that sunlight on your face. Just remember, it's eight minutes old.

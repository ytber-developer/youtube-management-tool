Coffee doesn't actually give you energy. What it does is sneakier. It blocks the signal telling your brain how tired you are.

The star of your cup is caffeine, and coffee doesn't own it. Caffeine occurs naturally in more than sixty plants, including tea leaves, kola nuts, and the cacao pods that give us chocolate.

Why would a plant make it? Scientists see caffeine as a plant defense chemical. Yet coffee and citrus flowers also slip a little into their nectar. In one study, honeybees rewarded with it were three times as likely to remember a flower's scent.

It works on bees for the same reason it works on you. Caffeine blocks adenosine receptors in the brain. Keep that word, adenosine, in mind, because it is the key to this whole story.

And we take in a lot of caffeine. More than eighty percent of American adults consume it. One review estimates the average person worldwide gets about seventy-six milligrams a day. In the United States and Canada, it's closer to two hundred ten to two hundred thirty-eight.

Start with the first sip. Caffeine is absorbed quickly from your gut into your bloodstream. From there it spreads into nearly every tissue in your body, and it slips easily past the barrier that protects your brain.

Caffeine levels in your blood usually peak within about an hour. Many studies put it at thirty to sixty minutes, but it can range from fifteen minutes to two hours, depending on your body and whether you've just eaten.

To see what it does next, meet adenosine. While you're awake, this chemical gradually builds up in your brain. The more it accumulates, the stronger your urge to sleep. Scientists call this sleep pressure, and NIH-funded research in mice suggests support cells called astrocytes help release it.

Adenosine works by docking onto receptors on your brain cells, like a key in a lock. Caffeine fits into those same locks without turning them. It blocks the receptors, so the tiredness message can't get through.

With that brake released, your brain releases more of its own stimulating chemicals, like dopamine and noradrenaline. You feel more alert and awake. But caffeine doesn't stop adenosine from building up. It only hides the signal.

So what does that actually do for you? Reviews find caffeine reliably sharpens alertness, attention and reaction time. Its effects on memory and on complex decisions are much less consistent. Coffee can make you quicker, but not necessarily wiser.

Your muscles notice too. Sports scientists find caffeine gives small to moderate boosts to strength, sprinting and jumping. Its most consistent benefit shows up in endurance exercise, like long runs and bike rides, though the effect differs from person to person.

The rest of your body reacts as well. Caffeine can raise your blood pressure. It increases the acid your stomach releases, which can lead to heartburn for some people. And it's a diuretic, so your body sends out more water.

That's where a famous myth comes from: that coffee dries you out. But Britain's National Health Service counts tea and coffee as part of your daily fluid intake, right alongside water. Your cup is still mostly water.

Coffee can wake up your gut too. In one small study, about three in ten people said coffee made them need the bathroom. In those people, colon activity jumped within four minutes, and even decaf did it.

Here's another myth: coffee stunts your growth. Harvard Health says there's no scientifically valid evidence for that. The idea may have come from an old belief that coffee causes osteoporosis, and it doesn't.

So how long does one cup last? The effects can linger for four to six hours. Caffeine's half-life, the time your body needs to clear half of it, is often three to five hours, but it ranges from about two to twelve.

Why such a huge range? Your liver does most of the work of breaking caffeine down, and its pace varies from person to person. Smokers clear caffeine almost twice as fast as nonsmokers, while pregnancy slows clearance down.

Your genes play a part too. Most of that work is done by one liver enzyme, and common variations in the gene behind it help explain why some people break caffeine down much faster than others.

That's why the same cup can leave one person buzzing and another ready for a nap. The FDA notes wide variation in how sensitive people are to caffeine and how quickly they get rid of it.

It also means an afternoon cup can still be working at bedtime. In a 2013 study, a large dose of caffeine taken six hours before bed still cut sleep by more than an hour, and people often didn't notice the difference.

How much caffeine is in your cup varies a lot too. MedlinePlus lists about ninety-five to two hundred milligrams in an eight-ounce cup of coffee. Even decaf usually has two to fifteen.

For context, the FDA cites four hundred milligrams a day as an amount not generally linked to dangerous effects in healthy adults. But it also suggests talking to your doctor if you're pregnant, breastfeeding, or have a condition or medication that might matter.

Too much caffeine can bring on a racing heart, anxiety, trouble sleeping and headaches. If coffee regularly leaves your heart pounding or fluttering, or you feel shaky and unwell, that's a good reason to check in with a doctor.

Drink it every day, and your body adapts. Regular drinkers develop some tolerance, so the same cup may seem to do less over time. Exactly how strong that tolerance gets is still debated by researchers.

Skip your usual cup, and you may find out how much you rely on it. Regular drinkers who suddenly go without may notice headaches, drowsiness, and irritability. The good news is that those usually fade after a couple of days.

What about the long run? A big 2017 review of more than two hundred analyses found coffee drinking was more often linked to benefits than harm, including a lower risk of type two diabetes. Pregnancy was the main exception.

But here's what scientists still don't know. Those studies show links, not proof. And roasted coffee is a mix of over a thousand compounds, so nobody is sure yet whether any benefits come from caffeine, from something else in the cup, or from the people who drink it.

And when caffeine finally wears off, adenosine can reach its receptors again. The sleep pressure that kept building all day is still there, waiting. So coffee isn't fuel. It's more like tape over a warning light.

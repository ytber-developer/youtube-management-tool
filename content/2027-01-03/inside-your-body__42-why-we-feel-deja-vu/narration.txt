You walk into a room you have never seen, and it feels familiar. Have you been here before, or is your brain playing a trick?

That odd feeling is called déjà vu, a French phrase meaning already seen. Cleveland Clinic describes it as a brief false sense of familiarity, as if a new situation has already happened to you.

Scientists define it as a short state in which something new feels familiar. Even while it is happening, most people know the feeling does not match the facts. They are sure they have never been there.

The good news is that it is very common. Estimates vary. Cleveland Clinic cites around ninety-seven percent of people, while another neurology source says about two thirds. Either way, most of us have felt it.

In one survey of healthy adults described in a scientific review, about seventy-six percent said they had experienced déjà vu. Another study of university students found about three in four had. You are in good company.

It also seems to change with age. According to Scientific American, young people report more déjà vu than older people, and one idea is that younger brains are more excitable.

So why does it happen? Here is the honest answer: scientists are not sure. Cleveland Clinic says researchers do not fully understand it. What we have are several ideas, and they are still being tested.

Most ideas begin with a part of the brain that handles memory, the medial temporal lobe, found just behind your temple. Scientific American says this area helps encode and retrieve memories and is tied to the feeling of familiarity.

The first idea is a memory misfire. Your brain may send out a familiarity signal for no good reason. Then another region checks your real memories, finds no match, and you are left with a strange feeling.

Cleveland Clinic neurologist Dr. Jean Khoury describes it as a misfire that blurs the line between memory and new experience, involving miscommunication between the temporal lobe and the hippocampus.

A second idea is about similarity. Imagine you are in a new hallway that happens to share a layout with a place you saw years ago, though you cannot remember it. Your brain feels the match but cannot name where it came from.

In a 2009 lab study led by psychologist Anne Cleary, scenes that secretly resembled earlier ones were more likely to give people the feeling of déjà vu. That supports the idea, though it does not prove it explains every case.

A third idea is that your brain is keeping you safe from mistakes. A researcher named Akira O'Connor suggests the odd feeling may look like an error, while actually being the moment your brain catches and avoids one.

Notice the pattern: every idea says déjà vu is a glitch in memory, not a message from the future or another life. It is a feeling, and your brain is the one tricking itself, which is a fascinating thing to be able to say about yourself.

Now, a reassuring point about everyday life. Dr. Khoury says that in healthy people it usually happens only a couple of times a year, and Cleveland Clinic says occasional episodes are generally harmless.

It may show up more when you are worn out. Cleveland Clinic links déjà vu with tiredness, sleep loss, stress, and anxiety, and also with migraines. So a busy week can make the feeling more likely.

Cleveland Clinic also says it may be more common in people with higher education, people who travel often, and people who remember their dreams. Researchers do not know exactly why, so treat that as a curious pattern.

Now the part to take seriously. Déjà vu can sometimes be linked to temporal lobe epilepsy, where seizure activity begins in that same memory region. In that case, the feeling comes from electrical activity in the brain, not from the surroundings.

Researchers who study epilepsy think the trouble may sit in parts of the temporal lobe near the hippocampus. In one experiment, electrical stimulation of a region called the entorhinal cortex was the spot most likely to trigger déjà vu.

Here is something that may surprise you. In a survey of people with and without epilepsy, déjà vu was reported less often by the patients than by healthy adults, about sixty-three percent versus seventy-six percent. So having it does not mean you have epilepsy.

So how can you tell a harmless moment from one that needs attention? Ordinary déjà vu lasts a few seconds. A seizure-related one may last a minute or two and come with other symptoms.

Those other signs include nausea, sweating, anxiety, repeated hand or mouth movements, or losing awareness for a bit. If you notice them, especially several together, a neurologist should take a look.

Cleveland Clinic says déjà vu that happens often, lasts more than a few seconds, or comes with confusion, headaches, loss of awareness, weakness, seizures, or shaking is worth a medical evaluation. A doctor can sort it out.

Doctors can look for other causes, too. Cleveland Clinic notes it can show up with migraines and with conditions like frontotemporal dementia, which is one more reason to get new or frequent episodes checked rather than guess.

But do not panic over a single strange moment. One odd feeling on a Tuesday is not a diagnosis, and most people who feel déjà vu are healthy. It is a curious quirk of a busy brain.

Researchers have another idea about age. Chris Moulin proposes that the frontal cortex acts as the brain's fact-checker, and with age it gets less effective at stopping false familiarity. Older adults still feel it, but may be less sure it is false.

Think about what that means. Your brain seems to have a built-in familiarity detector, and every so often, scientists think, it rings the bell at the wrong time. It is a tiny reminder of how complicated memory really is.

So the next time you feel it, take a breath and enjoy the moment. For a second or two, your brain mixed up now and then, and that says a lot about how clever, and quirky, it is.

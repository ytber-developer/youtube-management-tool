What if, right now, the Sun turned into a black hole? Would Earth get sucked in? The real answer is stranger.

Let's set the rules. With one impossible snap, all of the Sun's mass gets crushed into a black hole. Nothing is added and nothing is lost. The Sun holds about ninety-nine point eight percent of all the mass in our solar system, so this is a big squeeze.

How big a squeeze? The Sun is about eight hundred sixty-five thousand miles across. A black hole with the same mass would have a radius of just under two miles. You could drive across it in a few minutes, if you could survive the trip.

Here on Earth, you wouldn't notice a thing at first. Sunlight takes about eight minutes and twenty seconds to reach us. So for eight more minutes, the old light already on its way keeps arriving. The sky stays blue.

Then, all at once, the Sun goes out. Not a sunset. No fading. The daytime sky turns black, and the stars come out at noon. Everywhere on the day side of Earth, people stop and look up.

Moments later, the Moon goes dark too. It makes no light of its own. It only shines with reflected sunlight. So do Venus, Mars, and Jupiter. Over the next minutes and hours, the brightest objects in our sky wink out.

So now the big fear. Without the Sun, would Earth go spiraling into the black hole? Movies have taught us black holes are cosmic vacuum cleaners, swallowing everything nearby.

Here's the surprise. Nothing happens. Earth keeps orbiting exactly as it did before. NASA puts it plainly: if the Sun became a black hole of the same mass, the gravity we feel here on Earth would not change.

That's because gravity depends on mass and distance. Far outside the event horizon, a black hole pulls exactly like any other object with the same mass. The Sun's gravity never came from its size. It came from its mass, and that's still there.

The Moon still orbits Earth every twenty-seven days. The planets keep their paths. The calendar still works. A year is still a year. In NASA's words, the only thing that would change would be Earth's temperature.

And that's the real disaster. A black hole on its own gives off essentially no light. With nothing falling into it, our new black hole would be dark and silent. All the energy that warmed Earth for billions of years is gone.

At first, the cold creeps in slowly. Land, oceans, and air all hold heat. But scientists estimate that within about a week, the average temperature at Earth's surface would fall below zero degrees Fahrenheit.

Here's an eerie detail. The tides wouldn't stop. The Sun's gravity still helps raise our ocean tides, with a bit less than half the pull of the Moon. Even as the seas begin to freeze, they would keep rising and falling, right on schedule.

The seasons would keep coming too. Earth's tilt doesn't change, so each hemisphere would still lean toward the black hole in its summer. There would just be no warmth to receive. Summer would be nothing more than a date on the calendar.

After a year, estimates put the average surface temperature around minus one hundred degrees Fahrenheit. The air would be still and bitterly cold, and lakes and seas would be freezing over.

But the oceans have a trick. As the surface freezes, the ice acts like a blanket. It insulates the water below. Scientists estimate the deep oceans could stay liquid for hundreds of thousands of years under a lid of ice.

And Earth has its own heat. Our planet's hot interior keeps leaking warmth outward. Some microbes living in rock deep underground would likely carry on as if nothing had happened. They never needed the Sun.

What about people? Survivors would need power that doesn't come from the Sun: nuclear reactors, or heat from the Earth itself. Iceland already heats most of its homes with geothermal energy. Volcanic regions would become precious real estate.

One idea is submarines, parked in the deepest and warmest parts of the ocean under the ice. Life for humans would be cramped and fragile, but maybe possible, for a while.

Over millions of years, Earth slowly settles into a frozen balance, with the little heat leaking from its core matching what it loses to space. A dark, icy world, still circling a star that isn't there.

Now, let's say you could fly a ship to the black hole, with the snapped Sun at the center. Earth still sits about ninety-three million miles away, so you'd have a long trip. What would you see as you arrive?

Not much, at first. Just a tiny dark spot against the stars. But look closely. Its gravity warps space, so light from stars behind it gets bent around it. The background stars would seem to smear and ring around the dark spot.

At its center is the event horizon, the black hole's surface. Inside it, the speed needed to escape is faster than light. Nothing can come back out. This is the one place in our solar system you could never return from.

So could this ever really happen? No. Black holes like this come from giant stars. NASA says it takes a star of roughly twenty times the Sun's mass, or more, to leave behind a black hole when it dies.

Our Sun is far too small. In about five billion years, it will swell into a red giant, puff off its outer layers, and shrink into a white dwarf, a glowing ember about the size of Earth. No black hole required.

But this what if teaches something important. We often think the Sun keeps us alive by holding us in orbit. That part, a black hole could do just as well. What a black hole can't do is shine.

Everything you love about this planet, the oceans, the forests, the weather, your breakfast, comes from those eight-minute-old rays of light. Gravity keeps Earth in place. Sunlight is what makes it home.

A cold breeze, a scary movie, your favorite song. Totally different moments, same result: tiny bumps rising all over your skin.

Those bumps start at your hair follicles. At the base of each follicle sits a tiny muscle called the arrector pili. When it contracts, it pulls the hair upright and pushes up a little mound of skin around it.

Most of us can't decide to do this. It's run by your sympathetic nervous system, the fight or flight branch that also helps control your heart rate. That's why goosebumps can show up alongside a pounding pulse when you're startled.

For furry animals, this reflex is genuinely useful. When fur stands on end, it traps a layer of air close to the skin, a bit like a puffy jacket, and that air helps hold in body heat.

It also works as a bluff. A frightened cat puffs up its fur to look bigger and more threatening. Our distant ancestors had far more body hair, so the same reflex may once have done both jobs for them.

You, on the other hand, have thin, fine hair across most of your body. Raising it doesn't trap much warmth or scare anyone. So for a long time, goosebumps in humans were written off as a leftover with little purpose.

Then there are emotional goosebumps. Music, a moving film scene, or an inspiring speech can trigger a wave of chills that scientists call frisson. Exactly why strong emotions tap into this old reflex is still being studied.

In 2020, researchers at Harvard found a hidden second job. Working in mice, they showed that the nerve behind goosebumps also connects directly to the stem cells that grow new hair, wrapping around them like a ribbon.

The tiny goosebump muscle acts as a bridge that holds that nerve in place. In the short term, cold triggers goosebumps. If the cold lasts, the nerve fires harder and pushes the stem cells to start growing new hair.

That might explain why the reflex stuck around. It links a quick fix for a chill with a slower plan for a thicker coat. Whether it works the same way in human skin is still an open question.

Now for the strangest part. A small number of people say they can give themselves goosebumps on command, no cold or music required. Before 2018, science had described this in just three individual case reports.

That year, a study of thirty-two people with the ability found something curious. Music was the most common moment they used it, and as a group they scored higher than average on openness to new experiences.

So the next time your arms prickle during a song, think about what's happening: a reflex built for fur, wired into the cells that grow your hair, and still responding to the moments that move you.

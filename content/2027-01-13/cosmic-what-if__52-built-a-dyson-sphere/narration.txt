What if we built a Dyson sphere around the Sun? We would never be short of energy again. But could we really do it?

Start with the prize. The Sun gives off about three hundred eighty-three trillion trillion watts. That is the number in NASA's fact sheet. For comparison, humanity uses roughly nineteen trillion watts.

Divide one by the other and the Sun produces roughly twenty trillion times more power than all of civilization uses. And almost all of it flies off into empty space and is never used.

Earth catches only a sliver. By one estimate, about two hundred quadrillion watts of sunlight hits our planet. That is only about half of one billionth of the Sun's output. The rest just keeps going.

A Dyson sphere is the idea of catching it. The name comes from physicist Freeman Dyson, who wrote about it in a 1960 paper in the journal Science. The idea itself appeared even earlier, in a 1937 novel, Star Maker.

Dyson's point was practical. If a civilization kept growing, its power needs would keep growing too. At some point it might harvest a whole star. And that, he said, would be visible as a glow of infrared heat.

Movies and games love the solid shell, a giant hollow ball with the star inside. It makes a great picture. But here is a surprise: Dyson himself wrote that a solid shell around a star is mechanically impossible.

Part of the problem is gravity. Inside a hollow sphere, the pulls from all sides cancel out. That means the Sun would not hold the shell in place. Anything holding it still would need huge, careful forces.

Then there is the stuff you would need. Let's try some rough math. Mercury has about a third of a trillion trillion kilograms of mass, and it orbits about fifty-eight million kilometers from the Sun.

If you could spread the whole planet evenly across a sphere at Mercury's distance, you would get only about eight kilograms for each square meter. That is thin. And that is using every last scrap of the planet.

In other words, even a shell as flimsy as that would eat an entire planet. And it could not be a rigid, strong shell. It would be more like a thin sheet. So scientists look at a different design.

A Dyson swarm. Instead of one giant shell, picture countless separate collectors, each on its own orbit around the Sun. Dyson himself suggested this loose collection of objects as the realistic version.

The collectors could be solar panels, mirrors, or habitats. Each one catches some sunlight and sends the energy somewhere useful, perhaps beamed wirelessly to Earth. Nothing has to hold up anything else.

How would we build it? One proposal, discussed by Oxford researcher Stuart Armstrong, uses self-replicating robots. They would go to Mercury, where roughly half the planet could be mined, and build copies of themselves.

Armstrong estimated that this could take about fifty years, until Mercury's usable material ran out. Careful, though. This is speculation. It needs advanced automation and replicating machines that we do not have yet.

Now imagine it done. For the first time, energy would not limit us. Whole cities could be powered, and so could big space factories, and huge computers. Even our biggest projects would look small.

Getting the power home is its own puzzle. Proposals imagine beaming it wirelessly to Earth, or using millions of mirrors to focus light onto fewer collectors. Either way, countless parts would need to work together without crashing.

Scientists have a way to rank this. In 1964, Soviet astronomer Nikolai Kardashev proposed a scale of civilizations by the power they use. A Type One uses the energy of its planet.

A Type Two civilization captures the power of its star, about four hundred trillion trillion watts. That is exactly what a Dyson swarm would be for. A Type Three would use a whole galaxy.

Where are we? Carl Sagan fine-tuned the scale in the 1970s, and he rated humanity at about zero point seven. By 2021 our power use put us near zero point seven three. A long way from Type Two.

Still, a Dyson swarm would change the sky. Once collectors absorbed starlight, they would warm up and have to shed that heat. Dyson's paper pointed out that this would show up as infrared glow from far away.

That brings us to a real star with a strange story. In 2015, the Kepler space telescope's data, studied by citizen scientists in a project called Planet Hunters, showed something very odd about a star called KIC eight-four-six-two-eight-five-two.

Astronomer Tabetha Boyajian led the paper, so it is now called Tabby's Star. It sits about one thousand four hundred seventy light-years away, and its light dropped by up to fifteen percent in 2011.

In 2013, it dimmed by up to twenty-two percent. Planets cause only tiny dips, so something big was blocking its light. The dimming was irregular, with no clear pattern. It looked like nothing anyone had seen.

Some people suggested an alien megastructure, maybe a Dyson swarm in the middle of construction. Headlines exploded. But most scientists said the simpler explanations should come first.

Other ideas include a ring of dust, a swarm of broken comets, or planet debris. One researcher, Steinn Sigurdsson, called the alien option implausible, though still something you can test.

A 2018 study helped. Boyajian and colleagues found that the blocking material dims different colors by different amounts. A solid object would block all colors alike. So the dimming is most likely space dust.

Even so, as of the latest reviews, no single explanation accounts for everything about the star's light. So the mystery is not completely closed, but the alien theory has not held up as the best guess.

Back to our own Sun. A real Dyson swarm would take enormous effort, and it is very far beyond today's rockets. But as far as we know, nothing in basic physics forbids a swarm. It is an engineering and economic question.

And there are risks to wonder about. Block even a small share of sunlight, and Earth would get cooler. Any swarm would have to be built carefully, so that our own planet kept its warmth.

Dyson never claimed anyone would build one soon. He wanted to give astronomers a way to search for advanced civilizations by their heat. That, at least, is something telescopes can do right now.

So, what if we built a Dyson sphere? Not a solid shell, but a swarm, built from a planet's worth of stuff over decades. It would give us the power of a star, and move us up the scale.

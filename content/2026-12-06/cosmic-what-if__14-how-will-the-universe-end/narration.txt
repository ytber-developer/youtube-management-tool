Everything that has a beginning has an end. The universe began about thirteen point eight billion years ago. So how does it finish?

To find out, let's take a trip forward in time. Not years or centuries. We'll be skipping billions, then trillions, then numbers so large they barely mean anything. Along the way, the universe will change beyond recognition. And at the end, there's a twist that astronomers are arguing about right now.

First stop: our own neighborhood. The Sun is about halfway through its life. As it ages, it slowly gets brighter. Scientists estimate that within roughly a billion years, it will be hot enough to begin boiling away Earth's oceans. Life here has a deadline long before the Sun dies.

In about five billion years, the Sun runs low on hydrogen in its core and swells into a red giant, big enough to swallow Mercury and Venus. Whether it reaches Earth is still debated. Then it puffs off its outer layers and shrinks into a white dwarf, about half its current mass, roughly the size of Earth.

Meanwhile, the Andromeda Galaxy is heading our way. For years, astronomers said a collision was all but certain. But a twenty twenty-five study, accounting for the pull of other nearby galaxies, put the odds of a merger in the next ten billion years at only about fifty-fifty.

Even if it happens, stars are so far apart that direct collisions between them are rare. The two galaxies would slowly blend into one giant, rounded galaxy. Over time, our whole cluster of nearby galaxies is expected to merge like that. But that's not the strangest thing happening in the future sky.

Here's the stranger part. Everything beyond our local group is drifting away, and that drift is speeding up. In nineteen ninety-eight, two teams measuring distant exploding stars discovered that the expansion of the universe is accelerating.

Whatever is pushing it has a name, dark energy, but nobody knows what it is. NASA estimates it makes up roughly sixty-eight to seventy percent of the universe. According to NASA, the expansion began speeding up about nine billion years after the Big Bang.

If dark energy stays steady, distant galaxies keep fleeing faster and faster, until their light can never reach us. Eventually, astronomers in our merged galaxy would look out and see nothing but their own stars, surrounded by a dark, empty void.

Those galaxies won't be destroyed. They'll simply slip over a horizon. The space between us and them stretches so fast that their light can never close the gap. Nothing moves through space faster than light. But space itself is allowed to stretch faster than that.

And here's a genuinely unsettling idea. The faint afterglow of the Big Bang, the radiation that told us how the universe began, keeps getting stretched too. After several trillion years, it fades into insignificance. Future astronomers might never discover that there was a Big Bang at all.

Let's keep going. Our galaxy is still shining, but it is running out of raw material. New stars form from clouds of gas, and that gas is being used up. In one detailed study of the far future, the supply of star-forming gas runs out after about one to ten trillion years.

The longest survivors will be the smallest stars, red dwarfs. They burn their fuel so slowly that the tiniest ones can last around ten trillion years, hundreds of times the current age of the universe. Not one red dwarf has ever burned out yet. The universe is too young.

Around a hundred trillion years from now, ordinary star formation essentially ends. One by one, the last red dwarfs fade out. And then, for the first time since the early universe, the cosmos goes dark. Astronomers call what follows the Degenerate Era.

To put that in perspective, squeeze the entire history of the universe so far into a single second. On that scale, the age of shining stars would last about two hours. We are living in the first instant of it.

What's left are stellar corpses. White dwarfs slowly cooling toward black. Neutron stars. Brown dwarfs, failed stars that never ignited. And black holes. Every so often, two brown dwarfs collide and briefly spark a new star, a rare flicker in the dark.

Even galaxies don't last. Over immense spans, close passes between dead stars fling many of them out into intergalactic space, on a timescale of roughly ten quintillion years or more. Much of what remains spirals into the central black hole.

Then comes a question physics still can't answer. Are protons forever? Some theories predict that protons, the particles at the heart of every atom, eventually decay. Experiments haven't seen it happen, so if they decay at all, it takes far longer than the universe has existed.

If they do decay, then one estimate puts their typical lifetime around ten to the thirty-seventh power years. Over that time, every white dwarf, every neutron star, every rock and every planet would slowly dissolve into radiation and a thin haze of particles.

By then, the only big things left would be black holes. You might think those, at least, last forever. But Stephen Hawking showed that black holes slowly leak energy, a faint glow now called Hawking radiation. Very, very slowly, they shrink.

How slowly? A black hole the mass of our Sun would take far longer than ten to the sixtieth power years to evaporate. The biggest ones, billions of times heavier, could last around ten to the hundredth power years. That's a one followed by a hundred zeros. A googol.

To feel that number, give every atom in the observable universe its own clock, and let each one count off the entire age of the universe. Add them all up, and you'd still fall short of a googol years, by a factor of billions. Each last black hole would end in a final flash, and then be gone.

After that, there's almost nothing. A few scattered particles and faint, ever-stretching radiation, spreading through an ever-larger, colder void. No stars, no planets, no events. Physicists call this the heat death of the universe, or the Big Freeze. Not with a bang, but a long fade.

In a universe like that, nothing ever really happens again. No stars ignite, no planets form, nothing changes in any way you could notice. Some physicists argue that without change, even time itself would lose most of its meaning. The universe would go on, with no one and nothing to tell its story.

For a couple of decades, that was the favorite answer. But it all depends on dark energy staying constant. And here's the twist I promised. Recent measurements suggest dark energy might not be constant at all.

An instrument called DESI, on a telescope in Arizona, has mapped nearly fifteen million galaxies and quasars to trace how the universe expanded over billions of years. In twenty twenty-five, its scientists reported mounting hints that dark energy's influence may be weakening over time.

That isn't a discovery yet. Physicists measure confidence in units called sigma, and five sigma is the usual bar for a discovery. Depending on which other data you combine with DESI's, the evidence lands between about two point eight and four point two sigma. Strong hints, not proof.

Why does it matter so much? Because dark energy decides the ending. If it stays constant, you get the long freeze. If it weakens enough, some models allow the expansion to slow, stop, and maybe even reverse, pulling everything back together in a Big Crunch.

And if dark energy did the opposite, growing stronger over time, you'd get the most violent ending of all, the Big Rip. Galaxies would be torn apart, then solar systems, then planets, and finally atoms themselves. The current hints point toward weakening, which makes a Rip look less likely.

There's one more possibility, and it's the strangest. Some physicists think the empty space of our universe might not be in its most stable state. If a tiny bubble of a more stable kind of space ever formed, it could spread outward at nearly the speed of light, rewriting the laws of physics as it went.

Nobody knows if that can happen at all, and if it can, it may be so unlikely that it never does. You'd never see it coming, and there's no reason to lose sleep over it. But it's a reminder that we don't fully understand even empty space.

So, how will the universe end? Freeze, crunch, rip, or something no one has thought of yet. The honest answer is that we don't know, and the measurements that will tell us are being made right now, in your lifetime.

DESI keeps mapping. NASA's Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is designed to explore how dark energy behaves and has changed over time. Within the next several years, we may learn whether the hints are real, and with them, which ending the universe is heading toward.

And here's the thought to take with you. On the scale of that whole journey, we live in a tiny sliver of time when stars still shine, galaxies are still visible, and the light of the Big Bang can still be seen. We arrived at exactly the moment when the universe can still tell its own story.

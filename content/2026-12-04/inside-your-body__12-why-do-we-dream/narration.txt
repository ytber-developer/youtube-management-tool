Tonight, you will lie still, go nearly limp, and live through events that never happened. Scientists still argue about why.

Dreams are one of the most universal human experiences. Most people dream every night, even if they remember nothing in the morning. Yet for most of history, nobody could study them directly. Dreams happened in a locked room, and only the dreamer had the key.

That started to change in the early nineteen fifties at the University of Chicago. A graduate student named Eugene Aserinsky spent long hours watching the eyelids of sleeping people. He noticed that at certain times, their eyes darted rapidly back and forth beneath closed lids.

In nineteen fifty three, Aserinsky and his adviser, Nathaniel Kleitman, described this stage of sleep. It became known as rapid eye movement sleep, or REM sleep. And when they looked closer, they found it was linked to dreaming and to a surge in brain activity.

Since then, sleep labs have mapped the night in detail. Your sleep moves through stages. Stage one is the drift from waking into sleep. Stage two is true sleep. Stage three is deep, slow-wave sleep. And then comes REM.

These stages repeat in cycles. The cycle starts over every eighty to one hundred minutes, usually four to six times a night. Deep sleep is more common early on, and REM periods get longer as the night goes on. So many of your longest dreams likely happen toward morning.

REM is a strange state. Your eyes twitch. Your brain waves become almost as active as when you are awake. And yet your body is nearly paralyzed. Your muscles go limp, which keeps you from acting out the dreams your brain is producing.

Your brain chemistry flips as well. During REM, chemical messengers like acetylcholine and dopamine are more prominent, while others, including serotonin, norepinephrine and histamine, are suppressed. Some researchers think this mix helps explain why dreams feel so vivid and so strange.

And where does the dream material come from? One study suggests dreams draw more on imagination, meaning your memories, abstract thoughts and wishes, than on what your senses are picking up. Some researchers suspect the thalamus, a relay station for sensory signals, partly closes its gates while you dream. So your brain builds the scenery from what it already has.

Here is a clue about how dreams are built. Scientists believe your brainstem generates REM sleep, while your forebrain generates the dreams themselves. Damage to the brainstem can leave people dreaming without REM. Damage to the forebrain can leave people with REM, but no dreams.

And that hints at something textbooks used to get wrong. For decades, dreaming and REM sleep were treated as basically the same thing. They are not.

In two thousand seventeen, researchers at the University of Wisconsin had volunteers sleep in the lab wearing caps covered in electrodes. They woke them throughout the night and asked one simple question: were you just experiencing anything?

People reported dreams from REM sleep, but also from every stage of non-REM sleep, just less often. And in both cases, dreaming was linked to the same pattern: a drop in slow, low-frequency activity in a region toward the back of the brain. The team called it a posterior hot zone.

By watching that region in real time, the researchers could predict whether a sleeper would report a dream before they even woke the person up. Dreams, it turns out, leave a signature you can read from the outside.

So scientists now know a lot about when and where dreams happen. But that still leaves the big question. Why does your brain bother? Why spend hours each night building stories that you will mostly forget? There are several leading answers, and they do not all agree.

Start with the most famous. Sigmund Freud saw dreams as a pathway to the unconscious. He split a dream into its storyline and a hidden meaning underneath. It is a powerful idea, but Freud's claims have never been scientifically substantiated.

Modern sleep experts are blunt about dream dictionaries too. There is no consistent, scientifically proven way to link specific dream content to a specific meaning. A dream about falling does not have one universal translation.

A very different idea came from psychiatrists Allan Hobson and Robert McCarley. Their activation-synthesis theory says that during REM, the brain fires off signals, and the forebrain does its best to weave them into a story. In this view, dreams are the brain making sense of noise.

A third idea is evolutionary. Researcher Antti Revonsuo proposed the threat simulation theory. Dreams, he argued, are a kind of virtual reality where you can rehearse responses to danger safely. That might explain why so many dreams involve being chased, lost or unprepared.

A fourth idea focuses on feelings. Psychologist Rosalind Cartwright described dreams as a space where emotions are processed and regulated, linking what happened today to memories from the past. Unlike Freud's ideas, this view has found real empirical support.

It also fits everyday experience. Sleep experts note that stressful dreams are common when you are under stress. And people living with post-traumatic stress disorder are more likely to have recurring nightmares tied to what they went through.

The fifth idea is the one many sleep scientists consider the leading theory: memory. Dreaming may be a side effect of your brain sorting, strengthening and connecting what you learned, a kind of overnight filing system. And one clever experiment put that idea to the test.

In this view, dreaming helps consolidate and analyze memories, including skills and habits, not just facts. Some experts go further and suggest dreams may serve as a rehearsal for challenges you will face during the day, a practice run your brain stages while you sleep.

At Harvard Medical School and Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center, researchers had ninety nine college students learn a three dimensional virtual maze. Five hours later, the students were dropped into the maze again and had to find their way to a landmark.

In between, about half the students stayed awake. The others got a ninety minute chance to nap, and they were woken up and asked about their dreams. Only four of the fifty nappers said their dreams involved the maze.

But those four stood out. On the second try, they improved about ten times more than the nappers who did not dream about the maze. Simply thinking about the maze while awake did not predict any boost.

There was one more surprise. The nappers were woken before REM sleep could begin, so these learning-related dreams came from non-REM sleep. The group was tiny, so it is not proof. But it suggests dreams may be a window into memories being processed.

Which raises an obvious question. If dreams are so useful, why do you forget almost all of them? Sleep researcher Robert Stickgold has noted that people generally remember no more than ten to fifteen percent of their dreams.

Part of the answer is timing. You usually only remember a dream if you wake up during it or just after it. Otherwise, the memory never gets saved. And even when you do wake up mid-dream, it fades fast. That is why people who keep dream journals write the moment they wake.

So, why do we dream? The honest answer is that nobody knows for sure. The theories with the most support today point to emotion and memory. But dreams might do several jobs at once, or they might be partly a side effect of a brain that never fully switches off.

Science has mapped the stage. It has not yet read the script. What is certain is this. Every night, your brain stays busy while your body rests, replaying, sorting and inventing. The stories may vanish by breakfast, but the work happening behind them is one of the deepest mysteries your body holds.

Close your eyes and stand on one foot. Feel that wobble? That is your body juggling three hidden senses to keep you upright.

Try it near a wall, just in case. With your eyes open, standing on one foot feels easy. Close them, and suddenly you sway. That is because your brain was quietly using your eyes to help you balance all along.

Balance is a team effort. Your brain constantly blends signals from three sources: your eyes, your inner ears, and tiny sensors in the muscles and joints of your legs that report where your body is.

Take one away, like closing your eyes, and the other two have to pick up the slack. And the quiet hero of that team is hidden deep inside your head, just behind each ear.

Your inner ear does two jobs. One part, the snail-shaped cochlea, handles hearing. Right next to it sits your balance organ, the vestibular system, a tiny maze of curved tubes and pouches filled with fluid.

Each ear has five balance sensors. Three of them are curved loops called semicircular canals. The other two are little pouches called the utricle and the saccule. Each one measures a different kind of movement.

Here is test number two. Nod your head yes. Now shake it no. Now tilt your ear down toward your shoulder. Each of those three moves is picked up mainly by a different canal.

The three canals sit roughly at right angles to one another, like the corner of a box. Together, they can sense your head turning in any direction, whether you nod, shake, tilt, or do all three at once.

How do they feel a turn? Each canal is filled with fluid. When your head rotates, the fluid lags behind for a moment and pushes against a soft, jelly-like flap stretched across the canal, called the cupula.

Inside that flap are hair cells, tiny sensors with bristles on top. As the flap bends, the bristles bend too, and the hair cells fire off nerve signals to your brain. A turn of your head becomes electricity.

The two pouches handle a different job. They sense straight-line movement and gravity. The utricle notices moving forward and back, like a car speeding up. The saccule notices moving up and down, like an elevator.

And they do it with stones. Resting on their hair cells are tiny, dense grains of calcium carbonate, the same mineral found in chalk and seashells. Scientists call them otoconia, from the Greek for ear dust.

When you tilt your head, gravity tugs on those grains. They shift the gel they sit in, which bends the hair cells beneath. That is how you know which way is down, even with your eyes shut tight.

Now test number three. Hold up your thumb, stare at it, and shake your head side to side. Your thumb stays sharp. Now keep your head still and wave your thumb back and forth quickly. It blurs.

The difference is a reflex. When your head moves, your inner ear tells your eye muscles to roll your eyes the opposite way, by just the right amount, so your gaze stays locked on its target.

And it is astonishingly fast. In one study, people's eyes began correcting less than nine thousandths of a second after their head started to turn. That is far quicker than you could ever consciously react.

Without this reflex, every step you took would make the world bounce like shaky phone video. Cleveland Clinic notes that without it, you would likely become disoriented, and you could fall.

Now the classic, test number four. Carefully, somewhere safe, spin around a few times and stop. The room keeps swirling. Your feet are planted, but your brain insists you are still turning. Why?

When you start spinning, the fluid in your canals lags behind and bends the hair cells. But keep spinning, and the fluid catches up and moves along with you. The signal fades, and you feel almost normal.

Then you stop. Your head halts, but the fluid keeps swirling for a moment, like tea in a cup you just stopped stirring. It bends the hair cells the other way, so your brain thinks you are spinning the opposite way.

Your eyes get fooled too. That same reflex makes them flick back and forth, tracking a turn that is not happening, so the world seems to slide. Once the fluid settles down, the dizziness fades away.

Your balance senses can also get into arguments. Read a book in a moving car. Your inner ear feels every turn and bump, but your eyes, glued to the page, insist you are sitting perfectly still.

Scientists think motion sickness comes from exactly this kind of mismatch. Your brain expects your senses to agree, and when they do not, it reacts with yawning, sweating, a cold clammy feeling, and nausea.

Kids are especially prone to it. Babies under two rarely get motion sick, but it tends to peak between about seven and twelve years old, then usually eases as people grow up. Genes play a big role too.

A few tricks help your senses agree again. Look out at the horizon instead of down at a screen or a page. Keep your head still. On a plane, sit near the wings. In a car, being the driver helps.

Your brain can also learn. With repeated trips, it updates what it expects, and the queasiness fades. Experts call this habituation, and it is the most effective fix there is, even though it can be slow.

Sometimes the opposite happens. After a long cruise, some people step onto dry land and still feel like they are rocking. For most of them, that strange feeling fades within a few hours or days.

Those tiny ear stones can cause trouble too. Sometimes a few of them break loose and tumble into one of the canals, where they do not belong. Then that canal gets jostled whenever you move your head a certain way.

Doctors call this benign paroxysmal positional vertigo, or B P P V. It brings a brief but intense spinning feeling after a specific change in head position. It can follow a head injury, or simply develop with age.

The good news is that a doctor may treat it by guiding the loose crystals back out of the canal, using a careful series of head movements. One common version is called the Epley maneuver.

Balance problems are more common than you might think. In 2008, about fifteen percent of American adults, roughly thirty-three million people, reported a balance or dizziness problem. And the risk rises with age.

So talk to a doctor if you often feel unsteady, feel like the room is spinning, fall or nearly fall, feel faint, or get blurry vision or confusion with dizziness. It is worth getting checked at the first signs.

So the next time you jog down the stairs, glance over your shoulder, or just stand still, remember what is going on in your head. Fluid is sloshing, crystals are sliding, and hair cells are firing, all without you noticing.

Your balance is not one single sense. It is a nonstop conversation between your eyes, your body, and two tiny fluid-filled mazes hidden in your head, keeping your world steady and your feet on the ground.

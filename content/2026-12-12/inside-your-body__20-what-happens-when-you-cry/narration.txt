A sad movie ends, your throat tightens, and suddenly your eyes are wet. What exactly is your body doing when you cry?

Here is a surprise to start with. Your eyes are making tears right now, all day, every day. Crying is not when tears begin. It is when your tear system gets completely overwhelmed.

Your tears come from the lacrimal glands, small glands tucked under the upper outer corner of each eye. From there, the fluid flows across the surface of your eye and then drains away.

Scientists sort tears into three types. Basal tears keep your eyes moist all the time. Reflex tears rush in to wash away smoke, dust, or the sting of a freshly cut onion.

The third type is the star of this video. Emotional tears flow when strong feelings take over, whether that feeling is sadness, grief, or even overwhelming joy.

Every tear, of any type, is built in layers. There is an oily outer layer, a watery middle layer that is the thickest part, and an inner layer of mucus that helps it cling to your eye.

Tears taste salty for a reason. They are mostly water, but they carry dissolved electrolytes like sodium, potassium, calcium, and magnesium. A good cry really is a little salty stream.

So what flips the switch from an ordinary day to a full cry? It starts in your brain. A strong emotion builds, and your nervous system shifts gears before you even notice.

Cleveland Clinic explains that many emotions can activate your sympathetic nervous system, the same fight or flight system that fires up when you are stressed. That can trigger extra tear production.

Now your tear glands are making fluid faster than the older fluid can drain through your tear ducts. Picture a sink with the tap wide open and only a tiny drain. Something has to give.

So the extra tears spill over your lower eyelids and roll down your cheeks. That is the moment you would call crying. In a sense, it is a plumbing overflow, triggered by a feeling.

But not every tear escapes down your face. Your tear ducts empty into the back of your nose. During a cry, that flood heads there too, which is why crying gives you a runny nose.

Grab a tissue, because now your face joins in. Crying changes the muscles of facial expression. Your brows pull together, your mouth turns down, and your voice may crack or wobble.

Sometimes it builds into sobbing. Researchers describe sobbing as convulsive breathing in and out, with spasms of the muscles in your chest and belly. It can feel like your whole body is crying.

Inside, your body is now running two systems at once. Some signals rev you up, like a faster heartbeat. Others start calming you down, like slower breathing. Both happen during the same cry.

Here is the twist. In one study described in a research review, the calming effects lasted two to three minutes longer than the revving effects. Your body is quietly steering you back toward calm.

So does crying actually make you feel better? Most people say yes. In a survey across thirty five countries, most men and women reported feeling better after their most recent cry.

But when scientists measure mood right after people cry in experiments, they usually find people feel worse, not better. The review authors think the benefits need some time, at least several minutes, to develop.

So if you feel wiped out right after a cry, that is completely normal. The relief, when it comes, tends to arrive later, once your breathing settles and your body winds down.

Why would that happen? One idea involves your parasympathetic nervous system, the rest and digest side. Researchers propose that crying may switch it on, helping you settle after a wave of emotion.

Another idea involves oxytocin, a hormone linked to bonding and comfort, and possibly your body's own natural painkillers. These are still hypotheses, but they could help explain the calm after the storm.

You may have heard that crying flushes stress hormones and toxins out through your tears. It is an old and popular idea. But the review concludes that any soothing effect is not likely to come from that.

Instead, a big part of the power of crying seems to be social. Tears are a signal. Seeing someone cry tends to make people more willing to help and comfort them, even automatically.

That fits with how crying begins in life. Newborn babies cry loudly from day one, but tearful crying usually does not start until somewhere between four and eight weeks old.

A baby's cry is a call to bring a caregiver close. Researchers trace it back to the distress calls that young mammals and most birds make when they are separated from their parents.

And as far as scientists can tell, emotional crying is uniquely human. Plenty of animals call out in distress, but shedding tears because of feelings seems to be our own thing.

Context matters, too. Cleveland Clinic notes that crying will not have the same effect for everyone. Some people feel better crying alone, while others feel better when a loved one comforts them.

You can even cry when you are happy. Weddings, reunions, a winning goal. Strong emotion of either kind can trigger the same tear system, which is why joy and grief can look so alike.

So crying is not a malfunction. It is a full body response, from your brain to your tear glands to your breathing, and it may help both you and the people around you.

One honest note. If you cry so often that it disrupts your daily life, or you are crying more than you would like, it is a good idea to talk with a doctor or a mental health professional.

So next time a movie gets you, let it happen. Your eyes overflow, your nose runs, your breathing hitches, and a few minutes later, your body is already working to bring you back to calm.

What if a dark, wandering planet with no star of its own came drifting straight through our solar system? What would happen to you?

Planets like this are real. Astronomers call them rogue planets: worlds that drift through space without orbiting any star. Many were probably flung out of their home systems long ago, and now they wander the galaxy in the dark.

And there may be a staggering number of them. A NASA-backed study, built on a nine-year survey from a telescope in New Zealand, concluded that rogue planets could be about six times more common than planets that orbit stars.

One NASA scientist estimates our galaxy may hold twenty times more rogue planets than stars. They are found by microlensing. When one drifts in front of a distant star, its gravity bends that starlight and makes the star flicker brighter.

So let's imagine one heading our way. To make it dramatic, give it the mass of Jupiter. It is cold and black, with no light of its own. In our story it is already on its way, and nobody has noticed it.

Spotting it would be hard, because it only shines by reflected sunlight. One study of wandering planets estimated that a small one would only become visible once it came within about a thousand times Earth's distance from the Sun.

How fast would it come? Our first known interstellar visitor, Oumuamua, cruised through space at about fifty-nine thousand miles per hour. At that speed, crossing a thousand times Earth's distance from the Sun takes nearly two centuries.

So our first warning might come generations ahead. Astronomers would see a faint dot creeping slowly against the background stars, getting brighter year after year as it fell in toward the Sun.

Long before it reached the planets, it would plow through the Oort cloud, a vast shell of icy comets surrounding the solar system. Its gravity could knock some of those comets loose and send them falling inward.

This has happened before, with stars. About seventy thousand years ago, a small red star called Scholz's star passed roughly zero point eight light-years from the Sun, through the outer edge of that comet cloud.

Our ancestors were alive back then. The star was normally far too faint to see, but it is prone to flares, and researchers think it may have flashed into naked-eye view for minutes or hours at a time.

And another visitor is coming. In about one point three million years, a star called Gliese seven ten is expected to cut through the Oort cloud, far closer than Scholz's star did. Some comets it disturbs could eventually head our way.

Back to our rogue. Here is a key fact: a planet is far lighter than a star. Even Jupiter has only about one thousandth of the Sun's mass. So to really shake things up, our rogue would have to pass very close.

Its first targets would be the giants on the outskirts. Neptune, Uranus, Saturn and Jupiter are huge and spread far apart, and a close pass could tug their orbits into new shapes. That is where the real trouble starts.

In 2024, astronomers Nathan Kaib and Sean Raymond showed that passing stars change Earth's orbit mostly indirectly. They nudge the giant planets, and then the giant planets, over millions of years, nudge us.

Why would you care? Right now Earth's orbit is very close to a circle, so our distance from the Sun barely changes through the year. And the geologic record shows that when Earth's orbit grows more stretched, the climate swings with it.

On a more oval orbit, you would spend part of the year swinging close to the Sun and part of it far away. Sunlight would rise and fall through the year in a way it barely does now.

So what are the odds of something worse? A 2023 study simulated stars passing within a hundred times Earth's distance from the Sun. In about ninety-two percent of the runs, all eight planets survived on orbits much like today's.

But not every time. Mercury turned out to be the most fragile planet, often ending up in the Sun. And Earth, in rare runs, crashed into another planet, fell into the Sun, or was flung out into the dark.

Remember, those were stars, far heavier than a Jupiter-sized wanderer. A rogue planet would usually do much less damage. But let's imagine the rarest, worst ending anyway. Earth gets slung out of the solar system entirely.

Over the following years, the Sun would shrink in the sky from a blazing disk into just a very bright star. Daylight would dim, then fade away, and the warmth you take for granted would drain off into space.

The oceans would freeze over from the top down, and the surface would sink toward the deep cold of space. But the planet would not die inside. Earth's interior still makes its own heat, seeping up through the rock.

Scientists have actually studied worlds like this. Physicists Dorian Abbot and Eric Switzer called them Steppenwolf planets: lone wanderers that might keep a liquid ocean hidden under a thick shell of ice, warmed from below.

Their math suggests a planet like Earth would need extra insulation to pull it off, such as far more water or a thick blanket of frozen carbon dioxide. Without that, an Earth-sized world would likely freeze much deeper.

Life on the surface would be over. But the authors suggest that in a hidden ocean like that, life could in principle survive, riding a frozen planet between the stars, warmed only by the heat beneath its feet.

So should you worry? Not really. One classic study put the chance of a passing star throwing Earth out of the solar system within the next three and a half billion years at about one in four hundred thousand.

Rogue planets may outnumber stars, but they are so much lighter that most would likely sail through, doing little more than stirring up some comets on their way past.

And here is the twist. A cold, dark wanderer is already surprisingly close. An object called WISE zero eight five five, about twice Jupiter's mass and colder than freezing, sits just seven point four light-years away.

It is classed as a brown dwarf, a failed star, but it looks a lot like a rogue Jupiter, with no sun of its own. The James Webb Space Telescope has even spotted shifting water clouds on it.

So rogue worlds are not science fiction. There may be billions of them, almost all invisible. And every so often, the galaxy sends a visitor close enough to remind us that our solar system is not sealed off.

Earth spins so smoothly you never feel it. But what if it stopped? The answer depends on one thing: how fast it stops.

Right now, a spot on the equator is moving at about one thousand thirty seven miles per hour, carried around by Earth's spin. In New York City, you're moving at almost eight hundred miles per hour. At the North Pole, you're just turning in place.

So why don't you feel it? Because the motion is smooth and steady. On a jet cruising at five hundred miles per hour, you only notice when it speeds up or slows down. A sudden stop would be the ultimate slam on the brakes.

Scenario one: the solid Earth stops in an instant. The ground would halt, but anything not firmly attached to it would not. You, your house, the air, and the oceans would all keep rushing east at full speed.

Near the equator, the atmosphere would scour the surface at more than a thousand miles per hour. That's roughly five times faster than the strongest surface wind ever recorded, and with about twenty five times the force.

The oceans would surge out of their basins and pour across the land. Near the equator, almost nothing on the surface would survive. Your best odds would be close to the poles, where the ground was barely moving to begin with.

Fortunately, nothing we know of could stop a planet that fast. Earth's spin holds an enormous amount of momentum, and there's no brake big enough. So try scenario two: Earth slows down gradually, over a very long time, until it stops.

Here's the surprising part. Earth isn't a perfect sphere. Its spin flings material outward, so the planet bulges at the equator. Stand on the equator, and you're about thirteen miles farther from Earth's center than you would be at either pole.

The oceans are shaped by that spin too, piling up around the middle of the planet. Take the spin away, and gravity pulls the water toward the poles, which now sit lower than the old equatorial bulge.

Mapping experts at the company Esri modeled exactly this. In their simulation, the water gathers into two vast polar oceans, and a single giant continent rises around the equator, wrapping all the way around the world.

Much of North America would sink beneath a new northern ocean, with only the higher ground poking out. Meanwhile, the old seafloor near the equator would become dry land, so high above the new sea level that, scientists think, the air there would be as thin as on a tall mountain.

Then there's the light. If Earth stopped turning completely, a single day would last a whole year. Wherever you lived, you'd get about six months of daylight, followed by about six months of night.

And here's a strange detail. Even with no spin, Earth's trip around the Sun would slowly carry the Sun across your sky. Because of the direction we orbit, the Sun would rise in the west and set in the east, once a year.

The sunlit side would bake under a Sun that barely moves, while the dark side would freeze for months on end. The air would carry some of that heat around, but you should expect weather far more extreme than anything you've lived through.

Even the shape of the weather would change. Today, Earth's rotation twists moving air into the spirals of hurricanes and the curves of the jet streams. Without rotation, those patterns would break down, replaced by air flowing between the hot side and the cold side.

There's also an open question. Earth's magnetic field is generated by churning liquid iron in the core, and rotation helps organize that churning. Some scientists wonder whether the field would weaken without it. Nobody knows for sure.

Now the twist: Earth really is slowing down. Tides raised by the Moon and Sun act like a brake on the planet. NASA researchers found that a day has grown about forty seven thousandths of a second longer over the past thirty two hundred years.

That braking doesn't just disappear. The spin Earth loses is handed to the Moon, nudging it into a slightly bigger orbit. That's why the Moon is drifting about an inch farther away from you every year.

At that pace, Earth won't stop spinning anytime soon. Long before our planet could come to rest, the Sun itself will have run low on fuel and changed beyond recognition. So the slow stop stays a thought experiment.

So you can relax. The ground beneath you will keep carrying you east at hundreds of miles per hour, smoothly and silently, just as it has every second of your life.

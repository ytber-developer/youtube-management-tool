Right now, an organ about the size of a football is juggling more than five hundred jobs inside you. Can you point to it?

It's your liver. It sits on the right side of your upper belly, tucked under your ribs, and weighs about three to five pounds. It is the largest organ inside your body.

Cleveland Clinic counts more than five hundred important functions. That is far too many for one video, so let's do something different. Let's follow your liver through a single day, and watch it switch jobs.

First, you need to know about its unusual plumbing. Most organs get blood from one main supply line. Your liver gets two. About a quarter of its blood arrives through the hepatic artery, rich in fresh oxygen.

The other three quarters arrive through the portal vein, which carries blood straight from your stomach and intestines. So almost everything you absorb from a meal passes through your liver before it reaches the rest of you.

That makes your liver a checkpoint, a bit like a customs office standing between your gut and your heart. Every delivery gets inspected, sorted and processed. Let's watch the checkpoint at work.

One more thing before we start the clock. Your liver never clocks out. It doesn't get to rest while you sleep, and as you'll see, the night shift turns out to be one of its most important.

Seven in the morning. You eat breakfast. Sugars from your food flood into the portal vein. Your liver grabs a large share of that glucose and packs it away in a storage form called glycogen.

Think of glycogen as a pantry. Your liver stocks it after meals, which helps keep your blood sugar steady instead of swinging wildly, and keeps fuel on hand for later.

Noon. You eat lunch, maybe something with fat in it, like a cheeseburger. Fat and water don't mix, so your gut needs help breaking that fat down. The help comes from bile, a fluid made by your liver.

Bile is stored in your gallbladder and released into your small intestine after a meal. It works a bit like dish soap, breaking big blobs of fat into tiny droplets that enzymes can digest.

Bile is also a disposal chute. When old red blood cells are recycled, a yellow pigment called bilirubin is left over. Your liver sends it out in bile, and it ends up helping give stool its brown color.

If bilirubin builds up because the liver or its bile ducts aren't working well, it can tint the skin and the whites of the eyes yellow. Doctors call this jaundice, and it's a sign worth taking seriously.

Afternoon. In the background, your liver runs a protein factory. It makes albumin, the most common protein in your blood plasma, which helps keep fluid inside your blood vessels where it belongs.

It also makes clotting factors, the proteins that help plug a cut. Without them, even a small scrape would struggle to stop bleeding. Every scraped knee you ever healed from relied on your liver.

Now for a dirty job. When your body breaks down protein, it creates ammonia, a substance that is toxic to the brain in large amounts. Your liver is the only place in your body that turns it into urea.

Urea is far safer. It travels in your blood to your kidneys, which filter it out into your urine. Your liver and kidneys work as a cleanup team, all day, every day.

Evening. Maybe you take a medicine for a headache. Your liver contains enzymes that chemically change many medicines, alcohol and other substances, so your body can clear them out.

That is one reason doctors pay attention to the liver when choosing medicines and doses, and why heavy drinking over many years can harm it. The organ that cleans up chemicals can be damaged by them.

Your liver is also a guard post. Its blood channels are lined with special immune cells called Kupffer cells, which catch bacteria and debris arriving from the gut before they travel any further.

And it handles a huge volume of traffic. According to Cleveland Clinic, your liver filters more than two hundred fifty gallons of blood every single day.

Midnight. You are asleep, and you haven't eaten in hours. Your blood sugar would start to sag, so your liver opens the pantry, breaking glycogen back down into glucose and releasing it to fuel your brain.

If the fast goes on longer, your liver can even build brand new glucose from other raw materials, including pieces of proteins and fats. It is a backup generator that keeps your brain running.

Meanwhile, it makes cholesterol, which your body needs to build cell walls and some hormones. And it stores vitamins and iron, ready to hand out whenever your body runs low.

How does one organ juggle all of this? Its main working cells, called hepatocytes, are arranged in thousands of tiny units called lobules, each one a miniature factory with blood flowing past rows of cells.

Now for the most astonishing part. The liver is one of the only organs in your body that can regenerate. Lose a large piece of it, and it can grow back.

The ancient Greeks told a myth about Prometheus, who was punished by an eagle that ate his liver every day, only for it to grow back by the next. It's a myth, but the regrowing liver is real.

Surgeons rely on this. In a living donor transplant, a healthy person gives part of their liver, often more than half, to someone who needs one. The donor's remaining liver typically grows back to its original size within months.

Meanwhile, the transplanted piece grows inside the patient too, and both livers regain their function over time. Two people walk away from one liver. Few organs could ever pull that off.

Scientists are still working out exactly how this happens. In lab animals, when about two thirds of the liver is removed, the remaining cells start dividing and rebuild the lost mass within days to weeks.

Curiously, the liver regrows its size, not its original shape. The lobes that remain simply grow bigger, until the total amount of liver is right for the body it serves.

But this superpower has limits. Long term damage, from things like heavy alcohol use, some viral infections or a buildup of fat, can leave scar tissue that does not grow back into healthy liver.

In fact, Cleveland Clinic lists more than one hundred types of liver disease. That's a reminder of just how many delicate jobs this single organ has to get right, every day of your life.

The liver often keeps working quietly even when it's struggling. Cleveland Clinic advises seeing a doctor for yellowing of the skin or eyes, belly pain that won't go away, or weight loss you can't explain.

So the next time you eat a meal, sleep through the night, or heal from a scrape, remember the quiet chemist tucked under your ribs. Day and night, your liver is keeping you alive.

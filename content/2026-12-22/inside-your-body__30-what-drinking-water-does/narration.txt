Right now, more than half of you is water. So what actually happens inside you after you take a sip?

Doctors describe water as your body's chief ingredient. It makes up roughly fifty to seventy percent of your body weight, and the exact share depends on your age, your sex, and how much body fat you carry.

And it is not spread evenly. Classic measurements found your brain and heart are about seventy-three percent water. Your lungs are about eighty-three percent. Even your bones, which feel completely dry, are almost one third water.

So let's follow one ordinary sip. It starts in your mouth, and something surprising happens right away. Your brain begins to quiet your thirst while the water is still on its way down, long before it reaches your blood.

In studies of thirsty volunteers, drinking by mouth eased thirst within about five minutes, before their blood had changed at all. When the same water was delivered straight to the stomach through a tube, that quick relief did not happen.

Scientists think sensors in your mouth and throat give your brain an early estimate of how much you drank. That may help you stop at about the right amount, instead of drinking until your blood finally catches up.

Next, you swallow. The water slides down your esophagus and into your stomach. Plain water needs no digesting at all, so your stomach can pass it along quickly to the next stop, your small intestine.

That is where most of the action happens. The lining of your intestine is covered in tiny folds and finger-like bumps, and water slips through that lining into the tiny blood vessels just underneath.

How fast does it get there? Researchers labeled drinking water with a traceable heavy form of hydrogen and followed it through the body. The labeled water showed up in volunteers' blood just five minutes after they drank it.

About half of that drink was absorbed in roughly eleven to thirteen minutes. Within about two hours, nearly all of it had made it in. Your sip is no longer in your gut. It is part of your bloodstream.

From there, water spreads out everywhere. It moves into the fluid between your cells and into the cells themselves. Every cell and organ you have needs water to work, which is why your body guards its supply so carefully.

One of its first jobs is delivery. The water in your blood helps carry nutrients out to your cells, and it helps carry waste away from them, so it can later leave your body.

Water also keeps you running smoothly. It is the main part of your saliva, which helps you chew and swallow. And it is part of the fluid that lubricates and cushions your joints, so your knees and elbows can move easily.

It even works as padding. Water helps protect your spinal cord and other sensitive tissues, cushioning them against the bumps and jolts of everyday life, like hopping down a step or stumbling on the sidewalk.

Then there is heat. When you get warm, your body pushes water out through your skin as sweat. As that sweat evaporates, it carries heat away with it. It is your built-in cooling system, and it runs on water.

That is why you need more water on hot days, or when you work out hard. Every drop you sweat away has to be replaced, or your cooling system starts to run low, and you can begin to overheat.

Water helps your gut, too. It keeps food moving through your intestines, and it helps prevent and relieve constipation. A well-watered gut keeps things soft and moving along on schedule.

Eventually, your sip reaches the real water managers of your body, your kidneys. Each one is packed with about a million tiny filtering units called nephrons, and they never take a day off.

Together, your kidneys filter about one hundred fifty quarts of blood every single day. That is nearly forty gallons. But only about one to two quarts of it ever leave your body as urine.

Everything else gets pulled back into your blood. Your kidneys are constantly deciding how much water to keep and how much to let go, and they make that choice based on signals from your brain.

Deep in your brain, special sensors keep watch on how concentrated your blood is. If your blood gets just one to two percent more concentrated than normal, those sensors notice, and they spring into action.

First, your brain releases a hormone called vasopressin. It tells your kidneys to hold on to water, so your urine gets darker and more concentrated. If that is not enough, you start to feel thirsty.

That makes your urine color a handy, rough gauge. Pale yellow usually means you are well hydrated. Dark yellow and strong-smelling can be a sign that your body is trying to save water.

So how much should you drink? There is no single magic number. Health guidelines put total daily water for adults at roughly eleven and a half cups for women and fifteen and a half cups for men.

But that is total water, not just glasses of water. Mayo Clinic notes about a fifth of it usually comes from food. Fruits, vegetables, soups, milk, and even tea and coffee all count toward it.

Your needs also change with how active you are, the weather, your age, and some medical conditions. For most healthy people, a simple guide works well. Drink when you are thirsty, and choose water often.

Not every drink works the same way. Alcohol acts as a diuretic, which means it pushes your kidneys to release more water. That is why alcohol does not really add to your body's water supply.

If you fall behind, your body lets you know. Thirst, a dry mouth, a headache, tiredness, feeling dizzy, and peeing less often than usual can all be signs that you are getting dehydrated.

Usually, drinking more fixes it. But get medical help urgently if dehydration comes with confusion, a racing heartbeat, fast breathing, or dizziness that does not go away when you stand up.

Can you overdo it? It is uncommon. Cleveland Clinic notes that if your kidneys are healthy, drinking too much water almost never drives the sodium in your blood too low, unless you are already very dehydrated.

When it does happen, it is called hyponatremia. Warning signs include headache, nausea, muscle cramps, and confusion, and confusion or a seizure is an emergency. The lesson is balance, not chugging gallons.

So here is the big picture. The water in your glass right now will be in your blood within minutes, then in your cells, your sweat, and your joints. Then your kidneys will send some of it back out.

You are not a tank that gets filled up once. You are more like a river, with water constantly flowing in and flowing out, while your brain and kidneys quietly keep the level just right, all day long.

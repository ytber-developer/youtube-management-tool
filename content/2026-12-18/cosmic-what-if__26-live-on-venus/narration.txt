What if you tried to move to Venus, the planet next door that's almost exactly Earth's size? Let's find out how long you'd last.

On paper, Venus sounds like Earth's twin. It's about seven thousand five hundred miles across, compared to Earth's seven thousand nine hundred. And it comes closer to us than any other planet, about twenty-four million miles at its nearest.

So you pack your bags. The trip itself goes fine. The trouble starts when you look out the window. Venus is wrapped in clouds so thick that you can't see the ground at all.

And those clouds aren't made of water. They're made of sulfuric acid, the same nasty stuff found in car batteries. Underneath lies an atmosphere made mostly of carbon dioxide. Not one breath of it is safe for you.

Still, you decide to land. Your capsule drops through the clouds, and the air gets thicker, and thicker, and thicker. By the time you touch down, you're in a completely different kind of world.

First, the pressure. The air on Venus presses down about ninety-three times harder than air at sea level on Earth. Standing there would feel like being more than three thousand feet under the ocean.

A normal spacesuit is built to hold air in, against the empty vacuum of space. It's not built to keep a crushing ocean of air out. On Venus, it would fold like a soda can.

Then, the heat. The surface sits at about eight hundred seventy-two degrees Fahrenheit, hot enough to melt lead. Venus is the hottest planet in the solar system, even though Mercury is closer to the Sun.

Why so hot? That thick blanket of carbon dioxide lets sunlight in but traps the heat and won't let it escape. Scientists call it a runaway greenhouse effect, and Venus is the textbook example.

We know all this because the Soviet Union sent probes there. Between 1961 and 1984, its Venera program reached the surface ten times. The shortest-lived lander survived twenty-three minutes. The longest, about two hours.

The champion was Venera thirteen, in 1982. It kept working for one hundred twenty-seven minutes and sent back color pictures from the surface: flat, cracked rocks under a strange yellowish light.

So on the ground, a heavily armored robot lasts about two hours. You, in an ordinary suit, would last far less. Your life on the surface of Venus is over almost before it begins.

And even the calendar is cursed. Venus turns so slowly that one spin takes two hundred forty-three Earth days. But one trip around the Sun, a Venus year, takes only two hundred twenty-five.

Yes, a day on Venus is longer than its year. It also spins backward compared to Earth, so the Sun rises in the west and sets in the east. Going from sunrise to sunset would take about one hundred seventeen Earth days.

So the surface is out. But a NASA engineer named Geoffrey Landis noticed something. The problem with Venus, he wrote, is simply that the ground is too far below the comfortable part of the atmosphere.

Rise about fifty kilometers, roughly thirty miles, above the surface, and everything changes. Up there, slightly above fifty kilometers, the air pressure is about the same as at sea level on Earth.

The temperature at that height falls in the range where liquid water can exist, between freezing and about one hundred twenty degrees Fahrenheit. Gravity is about ninety percent of what you feel at home.

Landis called it the most Earth-like environment in the solar system, other than Earth itself. Not on the ground of some distant moon. Floating in the clouds of the hottest planet we know.

And here's the best part. On Venus, the air you breathe is a lifting gas. Our mix of nitrogen and oxygen is lighter than the heavy carbon dioxide around it, the way helium is lighter than air on Earth.

That means a balloon filled with plain breathable air would float. So your home could be the balloon itself. You would live inside the very thing that's holding you up.

Big balloons lift a lot. Landis calculated that a sphere one kilometer across, filled with breathable air, could lift about seven hundred thousand tons. That's roughly two Empire State Buildings.

Even a leak wouldn't be an explosion. Because the pressure inside and outside is nearly the same, the air wouldn't blast out. Gases would mix slowly through a hole, giving you time to find it and patch it.

The thick atmosphere above you would also shield you from space radiation, about as well as Earth's air protects you at home. That's a huge advantage over living on the Moon or on Mars.

NASA engineers have even sketched a mission. A study called HAVOC imagined astronauts living in an airship at fifty kilometers for thirty days. Fully inflated, the airship would be about as long as a Saturn Five rocket.

Of course, there are problems. The clouds are full of droplets of concentrated sulfuric acid. Anything left outside, especially the solar panels powering your home, would need a protective coating.

The HAVOC team tested coatings. One type of Teflon film still let through about ninety percent of the useful light, even after soaking for thirty days in concentrated sulfuric acid.

Then there's the wind. Near the cloud tops, it reaches about two hundred twenty-four miles per hour. A floating city wouldn't fight that wind. It would ride along, always drifting over new ground.

On the bright side, above the clouds there's plenty of sunshine for solar power. Your view would be an endless sea of pale yellow clouds below, and a Sun rising slowly in the west.

You would still need to make your own oxygen. But the air outside is mostly carbon dioxide, and machines can split oxygen out of it. The atmosphere also holds carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen and sulfur, ingredients life needs.

Now here's the haunting part. Venus may once have been far more like Earth. Scientists suspect that it started out with about as much water as our planet. Today, almost all of that water is gone.

Where did it go? Unlike Earth, Venus doesn't make a magnetic field to protect its air. Sunlight breaks water molecules apart high in the atmosphere, and the solar wind sweeps the pieces off into space.

A European spacecraft caught it happening. Venus Express saw about twice as many hydrogen atoms as oxygen atoms escaping from the planet. That's exactly the recipe for water: two hydrogens for every oxygen.

So could you live on Venus? On the ground, not for a minute. But thirty miles up, floating in a house made of breathable air, Venus might be the most welcoming place beyond Earth.

Deadly on the ground, and something close to home in the clouds. On Venus, everything depends on how high you're willing to float above it.

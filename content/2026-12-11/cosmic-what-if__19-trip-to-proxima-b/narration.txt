What if you packed your bags for the nearest planet beyond our solar system? How long would the trip take, and what's waiting there?

Your destination is called Proxima b. It circles Proxima Centauri, the closest star to the Sun. Astronomers found it in 2016 by watching the star wobble slightly back and forth, tugged by an unseen planet.

On paper, it looks inviting. Its mass is about the same as Earth's, maybe a little more. And it orbits in its star's habitable zone, where temperatures might allow liquid water on the surface.

The catch is the distance. Proxima Centauri is about four point two four light-years away. That means light itself, the fastest thing in the universe, needs more than four years to make the trip.

To feel that distance, think of Voyager One. It launched in 1977 and is the most distant spacecraft we've ever built. Just this fall, after nearly half a century of flying, it reached about one light-day from Earth.

One light-day is about sixteen billion miles. Proxima is more than fifteen hundred light-days away. So it's time to choose your ride. Let's go through the options, from slowest to fastest.

Option one: hitch a ride at Voyager's speed, about thirty-eight thousand miles per hour. That's fast enough to cross the United States in under five minutes. At that pace, reaching Proxima would take roughly seventy-five thousand years.

That's thousands of human generations. You'd need a ship that is a whole world, where people are born, grow old, and die, again and again, long before anyone arrives.

Option two: go much faster. The fastest object humans have ever built is NASA's Parker Solar Probe. Diving close to the Sun in late 2024, it reached about four hundred thirty thousand miles per hour.

If you could somehow hold that speed the whole way, the trip would still take about sixty-six hundred years. That's longer than all of recorded human history. Better, but still not a trip any one person could make.

Option three needs new technology. NASA scientists have pointed out that reaching just one tenth of the speed of light would bring a probe to the nearest star in about fifty years. That's within a single career.

Option four is the boldest plan on the table. A project called Breakthrough Starshot wants to push tiny probes, attached to ultra-thin sails, using powerful lasers on the ground. Light itself would do the pushing.

The goal is up to one hundred million miles per hour, fast enough to fly past the Alpha Centauri system just over twenty years after launch. But these would be tiny robotic probes. There'd be no room for you.

And even then, there's a delay. A photo taken at Proxima b would take more than four years to reach Earth. Ask a question and you'd wait over eight years for the answer. Conversations become letters between generations.

So let's cheat. Imagine you make the journey and arrive. The first thing you'd notice is the sun. Proxima Centauri is a red dwarf, with only about a tenth of the Sun's mass. It's cool, faint, and far dimmer than our Sun.

To stay warm around such a dim star, Proxima b has to hug it tightly. It orbits about four and a half million miles away, roughly five percent of the distance between Earth and the Sun. That's far closer than Mercury is to our Sun.

That tight orbit makes a year very short. Proxima b goes all the way around its star in about eleven point two days. If you're thirty years old on Earth, you'd be about a thousand years old in Proxima years.

Something else might feel strange. So close to its star, the planet may be tidally locked, with one side always facing the star. Astronomers say it may instead spin in a slow three-to-two rhythm. Either way, it's unlikely to have seasons like ours.

If it is locked, picture it. On one side, a red sun that never sets, hanging in the same spot forever. On the other, endless night. In between, a ring of permanent twilight, where some scientists hope conditions might be mild.

Now the bad news. Proxima Centauri is a violent little star. Its surface is turbulent, and it flares almost constantly. Its blasts of ultraviolet light and X-rays are much stronger than what Earth gets from the Sun.

In 2019, astronomers caught a flare that made the star about fourteen thousand times brighter than normal in ultraviolet light. It was over in moments, and then the star calmed down, as if nothing had happened.

And remember, Proxima b orbits about twenty times closer to its star than Earth does to the Sun. So every outburst hits it at point-blank range. Earth has a thick atmosphere and a magnetic field to protect it. Proxima b may not be so lucky.

Flares like that could strip away a planet's air over time. The lead scientist on that study said it's hard to imagine life emerging while being hit by radiation that often. Your spacesuit would need serious shielding.

So does Proxima b have an atmosphere? Oceans? Life? The honest answer is that nobody knows yet. We've never photographed the planet itself. We know it's there from the way it tugs on its star.

That's what makes it such a tempting target. It might be a scorched, airless rock. It might hide a thin strip of twilight where water stays liquid. The only way to know for sure is to look closer.

So here's your trip in summary. Seventy-five thousand years at Voyager speed. Thousands of years at the fastest speed we've ever reached. Decades for a laser-driven probe the size of a chip.

For you, Proxima b is still out of reach. But one day, a tiny probe riding a beam of light might fly past and send home the first close-up of a planet around another star.

Voyager took almost fifty years to travel one light-day. The nearest planet beyond our Sun is more than fifteen hundred light-days away. Next time you look at the night sky, remember how close the nearest one really is, and how far.

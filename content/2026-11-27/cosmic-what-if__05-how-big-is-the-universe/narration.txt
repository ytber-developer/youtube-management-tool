How big is the universe? To answer that, you'll need to zoom out, step by step, until even light starts to feel slow.

Start with light itself. It moves about one hundred eighty six thousand miles every second. That's fast enough to circle the Earth about seven and a half times in the time it takes you to say one Mississippi.

Sunlight reaches us in about eight minutes. Now take Voyager One, launched in nineteen seventy seven and the most distant spacecraft ever built. It is now about sixteen billion miles away, so far that a radio message from Earth takes roughly a full day to reach it.

Almost fifty years of flying, and it has covered about one light day. The nearest star beyond the Sun, Proxima Centauri, is about four and a quarter light years away. At Voyager's speed, getting there would take more than seventy thousand years.

So what is a light year? It's the distance light travels in a year, almost six trillion miles. If you drove that far at highway speed, never stopping for gas or sleep, the trip would take you about ten million years.

Now zoom out to our galaxy, the Milky Way. It's a spinning disk of hundreds of billions of stars, more than one hundred thousand light years across. Our Sun is just one of them, tucked into a quiet arm far from the center.

Our nearest big neighbor, the Andromeda galaxy, sits about two and a half million light years away. On a dark, clear night you can see it with your own eyes. The light you'd be catching left Andromeda before our species existed.

Here's where it gets dizzying. Starting in two thousand three, astronomers aimed the Hubble Space Telescope at a patch of sky that looked empty, about one tenth the width of the full Moon. They gathered light for about a million seconds. That blank spot held roughly ten thousand galaxies.

Those galaxies also reveal something else. The farther you look, the further back in time you see. Some of the faint smudges in Hubble's deepest images appear as they were less than a billion years after the universe began, because that's when their light set out.

To cover the whole sky with patches that small, you would need millions of them. Do the multiplication, and you reach well over a hundred billion galaxies. Some estimates go higher still, and astronomers are still refining the count.

Now the biggest number. The universe is about thirteen point eight billion years old, so you might expect to see thirteen point eight billion light years in every direction. But space itself has been stretching the whole time. The most distant places whose light we see are now about forty six billion light years away.

That makes the observable universe a sphere about ninety three billion light years across, with you at its center. Not because you're special. Every observer, in every galaxy, sits at the center of their own observable bubble.

And beyond that edge? Almost certainly more universe. We just can't see it, because its light hasn't had time to reach us. Some cosmologists think the whole universe is finite but enormous. Others think it goes on forever. As far as we know, nobody can say yet.

So how big is the universe, really? At least ninety three billion light years of it is visible. The rest is the biggest open question you can ask, and you're asking it from a tiny blue planet that light crosses in about a twenty fifth of a second.

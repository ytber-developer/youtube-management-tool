What if Earth were as big as Jupiter? Same rocks, same oceans, same you, just eleven times wider. Could you even stand up?

First, feel the scale. NASA says Jupiter is eleven times wider than Earth. If it were a hollow shell, about a thousand Earths could fit inside. Now picture our planet, every mountain and ocean, blown up to that size.

That alone makes it different from the real Jupiter. Jupiter has no solid surface at all. It is mostly swirling gas and liquid, and NASA says a spacecraft diving in would have nowhere to land. Our giant Earth would have solid ground.

And so much of it. Make a ball eleven times wider and its surface grows more than a hundredfold. That is over a hundred times the land, the ocean and the coastline. You could explore your whole life and never see most of it.

But here is the catch. Jupiter is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the lightest stuff there is. Earth is rock and metal. Gram for gram, our planet is about four times denser than Jupiter.

So keep Earth's density and inflate it to Jupiter's size, and you get a planet with about thirteen hundred times Earth's mass. The real Jupiter has about three hundred eighteen. Our giant Earth would outweigh Jupiter roughly four to one.

Surface gravity would climb with it. For planets with the same density, the pull at the surface grows in step with their width. Eleven times wider means roughly eleven times the gravity you feel right now.

If you weigh one hundred fifty pounds today, you would weigh about sixteen hundred fifty there. Standing up would be like carrying ten more copies of yourself on your shoulders. A gallon of milk would weigh almost a hundred pounds.

Could your body handle it? Studies of pilots show that people tend to lose consciousness at around four times normal gravity, as blood drains away from the brain. Eleven times is far beyond that, every second of every day.

Standing would be out of the question. Even lying flat, every breath would mean lifting a chest that weighs eleven times what it should. It is very hard to imagine a body like yours lasting long there.

And a fall would be brutal. Trip over your own feet and you would hit the ground more than three times faster than on Earth, with eleven times the energy. A simple stumble could be as dangerous as falling off a roof.

Any life that evolved there would probably stay low and tough. Even on today's Earth, gravity is part of what limits how tall a tree can grow. Under eleven times the pull, a forest might look more like a carpet of moss.

When researchers measured the tallest redwoods in 2004, the record holder stood about three hundred seventy feet tall, and lifting water that high against gravity was already pushing the limit. Multiply that gravity by eleven, and the ceiling would collapse.

But wait. Would it even look like Earth? Probably not. Planets are born inside a disk of gas and dust around a young star, and a big enough rocky core starts pulling that gas onto itself.

Some scientists estimate that once a core reaches about ten Earth masses, its gravity can collect huge amounts of hydrogen and helium. That is roughly how Jupiter itself may have formed. Our rocky giant would be more than a hundred times past that line.

So a real rocky world that big would most likely wrap itself in a deep blanket of hydrogen and helium. It would become a gas giant of its own, with the rocky Earth buried far down inside it, out of sight.

Observations back this up. A study of hundreds of planets, moons and stars found that above roughly twice Earth's mass, worlds tend to stop being rocky and start puffing up with gas. Big rocky planets appear to be rare.

Here is something stranger still. Add mass to a giant planet and it does not just keep getting wider. Gravity squeezes the inside harder. Some gas giants are even wider than brown dwarfs that weigh many times more.

That is what makes our rocky giant so odd. It would have Jupiter's width but about four times Jupiter's mass, packed with rock and metal instead of gas. Nothing like it is known in our solar system.

So would all that mass turn it into a star? No. Objects need around thirteen times Jupiter's mass to fuse even deuterium, a heavy kind of hydrogen, and about eighty to shine as a true star.

At about four Jupiters, our giant Earth falls far short. And the Sun would still be the boss. It has about a thousand times Jupiter's mass, so it would outweigh even our monster Earth roughly two hundred fifty to one.

Now suppose someone did live on this world. Could they ever reach space? For planets of the same density, escape speed also grows with width. It would be about eleven times Earth's, more than twice the escape speed of Jupiter itself.

Astrophysicist Michael Hippke calculated that chemical rockets become unrealistic on planets above about ten Earth masses, because the fuel needed grows exponentially. Our giant Earth is more than a hundred times past that limit.

Its people would be trapped by their own planet. Hippke suggests that heavy worlds would need something entirely different, such as nuclear propulsion. No ordinary rocket like ours would ever climb out of that gravity well.

Its neighbors would notice it too. A planet with four Jupiters of mass, orbiting where Earth does, would pull on Venus and Mars far harder than our real Earth ever has, and our Moon would have to race around it much faster to stay in orbit.

So do worlds like this exist anywhere? Astronomers have found thousands of planets around other stars, many of them bigger than Earth. But the giants they find are almost always wrapped in gas, like Jupiter and Saturn, not solid rock.

So here is the surprise. A Jupiter-sized Earth would not be a roomier home. It would be a crushing world you could never stand up on or escape from, or more likely, a gas giant with our planet hidden at its core.

Earth's modest size is part of why you can stand up, breathe easily, grow tall trees and send rockets to other worlds. Sometimes being small is exactly what makes a planet just right.

Two hundred forty miles above your head, the human body starts rebuilding itself. Within two days, you could even be taller.

Every system in your body grew up under one constant force: gravity. It pulls your blood toward your feet, presses down on your spine, and loads your bones every time you stand. Now imagine that force switched off.

That is daily life on the International Space Station, which orbits about two hundred forty miles above Earth. Astronauts there are not weightless because gravity is gone. They are falling around the planet nonstop, so their bodies never feel their own weight.

The first change happens fast. On Earth, your body constantly works to keep fluid from pooling in your legs. In orbit, that system keeps pushing upward, with nothing pulling back. So body fluids shift toward your chest and head.

Your spine changes too. With no weight pressing down, your back relaxes and lengthens. NASA research found that in as little as two days, a typical crew member can grow up to three percent taller. For a six foot person, that is about two inches.

But that bonus height is temporary, and it comes with a catch. Your body is a careful accountant. Whatever it stops using, it stops paying to maintain. And in weightlessness, it stops using a lot.

Start with your bones. They are living tissue, constantly broken down and rebuilt to match the loads they carry. In space, weight-bearing bones like your hips and legs lose on average one to one and a half percent of their mineral density every month.

Over a six month mission, that adds up. And those minerals have to go somewhere. Extra calcium leaves the body through the kidneys, and together with dehydration, that raises the risk of kidney stones.

Your muscles follow the same logic. On Earth, your legs and back fight gravity all day just to keep you upright. In orbit, they barely work. Without exercise and good nutrition, astronauts lose muscle mass faster than they would on the ground.

So astronauts train like it is their job, because it is. Crew members average about two hours of exercise every day, on a treadmill, a stationary bike, and a resistance machine that uses pistons and a flywheel to mimic lifting weights where nothing weighs anything.

Bones and muscles you might see coming. But some of the strangest changes happen deep inside your cells. And NASA had the perfect way to measure them: an astronaut with an identical twin.

Astronaut Scott Kelly spent nearly a year aboard the space station. His identical twin, Mark Kelly, also an astronaut, stayed on Earth. Because the brothers share the same genes, ten research teams could compare them and isolate what spaceflight itself had changed.

Scott's gene activity shifted in orbit as his body adapted. His gut bacteria changed. His telomeres, the protective caps on the ends of chromosomes, behaved in unexpected ways. And researchers saw signs of inflammation and a thickening of the wall of his carotid artery, in his neck.

Even his appetite surprised the scientists. Scott ate about thirty percent fewer calories than researchers had anticipated, and his body mass dropped by seven percent during the flight.

Then there are the eyes. That upward shift of fluid may press on the eyes, and some astronauts come home with changes to their vision. In space, Scott's blood showed higher levels of a protein involved in how the body handles water.

Here is the part most people never think about. For many astronauts, one of the hardest moments of the whole mission is not launch. It is coming home.

After months in orbit, your heart and blood vessels have adjusted to a world where blood does not drain toward your feet. Returning astronauts can feel lightheaded when they stand, and some even faint.

Your brain needs time to readjust too. In the Twins Study, Scott's thinking speed and accuracy held steady in orbit, but they dipped after landing, and some of that decline was still there six months later.

The good news is how much bounces back. About ninety one percent of Scott's gene expression changes returned to normal after he landed. His gut bacteria, weight and immune responses largely recovered, and a flu vaccine he received in space worked just as it should.

A few changes did linger, though, and bone is the slow one. NASA notes that bone lost in space may not fully return, even with rehabilitation.

And one hazard cannot be solved with a treadmill. Space is filled with radiation, including galactic cosmic rays that are very hard to shield against. The health risks are mostly long term, and researchers believe some DNA damage seen in Scott came from radiation exposure.

Now stretch everything you just heard into a trip to Mars. Mars is on average about one hundred forty million miles away. The journey takes about six months of weightlessness, and a message home can take up to twenty minutes each way.

Once you land, gravity is only about one third of Earth's, and each day is about thirty seven minutes longer. Future missions to the Moon and deep space could last up to three years.

And that is the real takeaway. Your body is not a fixed machine. It is constantly reading the forces around it and rebuilding itself to match. Take gravity away, and it adapts within days. Bring gravity back, and it slowly adapts again.

Which means that right now, just by standing and walking around, you are giving your bones and muscles a reason to stay strong. Gravity is not only holding you down. It is quietly keeping you built.

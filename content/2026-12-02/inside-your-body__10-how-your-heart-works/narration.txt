Since you woke up this morning, your heart has beaten tens of thousands of times. You never asked it to. So who is in charge?

Here is the scale of the job. Your heart beats more than one hundred thousand times a day. Every minute, it pumps about one and a half gallons of blood. By bedtime, that adds up to more than two thousand gallons, enough to fill a small swimming pool.

And it pushes that blood through an enormous network. If you lined up every blood vessel in your body end to end, they would stretch about sixty thousand miles. That is more than twice around the Earth.

The machine doing all this is surprisingly small. An average adult heart weighs about ten ounces, a little less than a full can of soda, and is about the size of your two hands clasped together. It sits in your chest, just behind and slightly left of your breastbone.

To understand how it works, you need three ideas: the pumps, the doors, and the spark. Start with the pumps, because the first surprise is that your heart is not really one pump at all.

It is two pumps, side by side. Your heart has four chambers. On each side, there is an upper chamber called an atrium, which receives blood, and a lower chamber called a ventricle, which pumps it out. The right side and the left side have very different jobs.

The right side handles used blood. Oxygen-poor blood returning from your body flows into the right atrium, drops into the right ventricle, and gets pushed out to your lungs. There, your lungs load it with fresh oxygen.

Then that oxygen-rich blood comes back to the left atrium, drops into the left ventricle, and gets launched into your aorta, the largest artery in your body. From there it travels everywhere, from your brain to your toes.

That is why the left ventricle is the powerhouse. Sending blood next door to your lungs is easy. Sending it all the way to your feet and back takes far more pressure, and the left ventricle generates the high pressure needed to do it.

Next, the doors. A pump is useless if blood can slosh backward. So your heart has four valves, flaps of tissue that open to let blood through and snap shut to stop it from flowing back the wrong way.

Between each atrium and its ventricle sits a valve: the tricuspid on the right and the mitral on the left. And at each exit there is another: the pulmonary valve, leading to the lungs, and the aortic valve, leading to the body.

Those doors are what you hear in a heartbeat. The first sound, lub, is the valves between the atria and ventricles closing. The second sound, dub, is the pulmonary and aortic valves closing. Lub, dub. That is the sound of doors shutting, about once a second.

Pumps and doors explain how blood moves. But they do not explain the most remarkable part. Something has to tell all of that muscle exactly when to squeeze, in the right order, every single second of your life.

Here is the answer, and it may surprise you. Every heartbeat begins inside the heart itself. Your heart has its own electrical wiring, called the cardiac conduction system, which controls the rate and rhythm of every beat.

The spark starts in a small cluster of pacemaker cells called the sinoatrial node, or SA node, tucked into the wall of the right atrium. It fires an electrical signal that spreads across both atria, and they squeeze, pushing blood down into the ventricles.

Then comes a clever pause. The signal reaches the atrioventricular node, or AV node, between the upper and lower chambers. It slows the signal down slightly, giving the ventricles time to finish filling with blood.

Then the signal races down special pathways, the bundle of His, the bundle branches, and the Purkinje fibers, spreading through the walls of the ventricles. They contract, blood surges out, and the whole cycle starts again at the SA node.

Your nervous system can speed this rhythm up or slow it down, but it does not create it. For a resting adult, a normal rhythm is about sixty to one hundred beats per minute. On average, women's hearts beat about eight beats per minute faster than men's.

So your heart pumps for every organ in your body. But here is a question most people never ask. The heart is a muscle too, working harder than any other. Who feeds the heart?

Surprisingly, not the blood rushing through its chambers. Instead, the heart has its own private supply lines, the coronary arteries. They branch off the aorta right at the start of its journey and wrap around the outside of the heart, delivering oxygen and nutrients to the muscle.

Coronary veins then collect the used blood and return it to the right atrium, so it can head to the lungs like everything else. The heart takes care of itself first, before sending a single drop to the rest of you.

Now for the last mystery. Your skin replaces itself constantly. So does the lining of your gut. But what about the heart, a muscle that never gets a day off? For a long time, nobody could find out. Then scientists found a clue in an unlikely place: Cold War nuclear tests.

Above-ground nuclear tests sharply raised the amount of a form of carbon called carbon fourteen in the atmosphere. That carbon ended up in plants, in food, and in the DNA of every new cell people made. Because the levels changed year by year, a cell's carbon reveals roughly when it was born.

In two thousand nine, researchers at Sweden's Karolinska Institutet used this timestamp on human heart cells. They found that heart muscle cells do renew, but slowly. About one percent are replaced each year at age twenty, falling to about half a percent a year by age seventy five.

That means most of your heart muscle cells will never be replaced in your lifetime. The heart beating in your chest right now is a mix of cells you were born with and cells formed later. Some of them have been beating, without a break, since before you were born.

So who is in charge of your heartbeat? Your heart is. Two pumps, four one-way doors, and a tiny cluster of pacemaker cells firing on their own, more than one hundred thousand times a day, for your entire life.

Next time you feel your pulse, remember what you are actually feeling: a spark, a pause, a squeeze, and two doors shutting. Over a lifetime, it adds up to billions of beats, and it has not missed its cue yet.

What if you were standing on Mars when a dust storm started to swallow the entire planet?

You are outside your base in a pressure suit. The sky is its usual butterscotch color. Then, far away, the horizon starts to blur. An orange wall is rising. Your first instinct says run. But should you?

In the movie The Martian, a storm rips the crew's equipment apart and hurls an astronaut through the air. It is a thrilling scene. According to NASA scientists, it is also the least realistic part of the story.

Here is why. The air on Mars is only about one percent as dense as the air on Earth. Even in the strongest storms, winds top out at around sixty miles per hour.

Sixty miles per hour sounds scary. On Earth, it would bend trees. But in air that thin, the wind carries very little punch. One NASA scientist said it would feel more like a gentle six mile per hour breeze.

So you will not be blown away. Your base will not be torn apart either. The real danger of a Martian dust storm is something much quieter, and it starts with the dust itself.

Martian dust is incredibly fine. Scientists estimate the average grain may be as small as three micrometers across, far thinner than a human hair. It floats easily, even in that weak, thin wind.

How does a storm grow? Sunlight warms the dust floating in the air. Warmer air changes the winds. Stronger winds lift even more dust, which absorbs even more sunlight. The storm feeds itself.

In 2018, scientists watched it happen. On May thirtieth, they spotted a regional storm, roughly the size of a small American state. By June twentieth, it had wrapped around the entire planet.

Storms that big do not happen every year. NASA says planet-wide storms show up roughly every six to eight Earth years. Researchers put the chance of one in any given Martian year at about one in four.

They tend to strike during spring and summer in the southern hemisphere of Mars. And here is the strange part: scientists still do not fully understand why some small storms go global and others fizzle.

Your warning would come from orbit. During the 2018 storm, NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter and Mars Odyssey tracked temperatures in the air and on the ground as the dust spread. A forecast like that gives you time to get ready.

Back to you. The orange wall reaches your base. At first, it is just haze. Then the Sun turns into a dim, pale disk. Within days, the middle of the day looks like late evening.

Scientists measure this darkness with a number called tau. At that time of the Martian year, it usually sits around one or lower. In June 2018, the Opportunity rover measured ten point eight, the highest ever recorded on the Martian surface.

That much dust turned day into night around the rover. And Opportunity had a problem you would share. It ran on sunlight. Its solar panels were its only source of power.

Opportunity was designed to last about ninety Martian days. Instead it had explored Mars for nearly fifteen years. Now, with its batteries draining, engineers put it into a deep sleep to wait out the storm.

The rover's last message reached Earth on June tenth, 2018. Then, silence. Engineers hoped that when the skies cleared, the sunlight would wake it up again.

Over about eight months, they sent more than a thousand commands, asking it to call home. Dust had likely coated its panels. In February 2019, NASA declared one of the most successful missions in history over.

Meanwhile, the Curiosity rover kept working right through the storm. The difference? Curiosity is nuclear powered. It does not care whether the Sun is shining. That is lesson number one for your base.

Lesson number two is patience. The 2018 storm lasted for months. In July, NASA expected the haze over Opportunity might not clear enough until September, based on an earlier global storm in 2001.

While you wait, something surprising happens to the weather. The dust acts like a blanket. It traps heat from the Sun, so nights do not get as cold as usual. Daytime, under the gloom, is cooler.

Even the dust devils change. Those spinning whirlwinds form when the ground is warmer than the air above it. With less sunlight reaching the surface, scientists expect fewer of them during a big storm.

Now the dust starts creeping into your life. Tiny grains coat your visor, your gloves, the joints of your suit. Every time you come back inside, some of that dust wants to come in with you.

That matters, because Martian dust is not just dirt. A 2025 study warned that it contains toxic ingredients, including perchlorates, silica, and fine iron oxides. Perchlorates can interfere with the thyroid gland.

Because the grains are so small, they could reach deep into your lungs. A NASA working group suggested an early limit of just a tenth of a milligram of fine Martian dust per cubic meter of air.

So your airlock becomes the most important room in the base. Brush off, seal up, filter the air. In a Martian dust storm, the enemy is not the wind. It is the stuff the wind leaves behind.

But the biggest surprise of all happens far above your head, and it is happening to the whole planet. NASA's MAVEN orbiter caught it during that same storm in 2018.

Normally, most water vapor on Mars stays low in the atmosphere. During the storm, the heated, dusty air carried it much higher. Over two days in June, MAVEN saw about twenty times more water up there than usual.

Up that high, sunlight breaks water molecules apart. The light hydrogen atoms escape into space and never come back. Mars lost as much water in forty-five days of that storm as it normally loses in a whole Martian year.

A Martian year is about two Earth years long. So in a few weeks, one storm drained two years' worth of water. Scientists think storms like this helped turn a once-wet world into a desert.

Finally, the storm fades. The dust settles out of the sky over weeks, leaving a fresh orange coating on everything. Your solar panels, if you have them, will need a serious cleaning.

Here Mars sometimes helps. In 2005, a dust devil swept over NASA's Spirit rover and cleaned its solar panels. Its noon power loss dropped from about forty percent to just seven percent.

And then you get your reward. As evening comes, you step outside and watch the Sun go down. On the red planet, the sky around the setting Sun glows blue.

It is that same fine dust at work. The grains are just the right size to let blue light pass through the air a little more easily, so the glow around the Sun turns blue. NASA's Curiosity rover photographed it in 2015.

So, could you survive a Martian dust storm? The wind will not hurt you. The darkness, the dust, and the long wait might. And while you hide, the storm is quietly stealing a little more of Mars' water.

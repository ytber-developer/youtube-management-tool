Picture yourself stepping off a spaceship toward Jupiter, ready to plant your boots on the biggest planet in the solar system. There's just one problem.

Jupiter has no surface. None. It's a giant ball made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the same ingredients that make up stars. What looks like ground in photos is just the tops of clouds, painted into stripes by winds and storms.

And it is enormous. Jupiter is about eleven times wider than Earth. Hollow it out, and about a thousand Earths could fit inside. It holds more than twice the material of every other planet, moon, asteroid, and comet in the solar system combined.

So with star ingredients and that much mass, why isn't Jupiter a star? It simply never grew massive enough to ignite. No nuclear fire ever switched on in its heart. It's a planet built from star stuff that stopped short.

Your trouble starts before you even reach the clouds. Jupiter's magnetic field is sixteen to fifty four times as powerful as Earth's. Near the planet, it traps radiation intense enough to damage spacecraft.

That's why NASA's Juno spacecraft, orbiting Jupiter since 2016, keeps its most sensitive electronics inside a titanium vault about the size of an SUV's trunk. The vault alone weighs four hundred pounds. Your suit would need to be at least that tough.

And Jupiter takes hits. In July 1994, more than twenty pieces of a shattered comet slammed into it over six days. For the first time in history, NASA had spacecraft in position to watch two bodies in our solar system collide.

The fragments struck at about thirty seven miles per second, heating the air to at least fifty three thousand degrees Fahrenheit. Dark, ringed scars spread across the clouds and stayed visible for months, until Jupiter's winds finally smeared them away.

Your turn. When NASA's Galileo probe dropped into Jupiter in nineteen ninety five, it hit the atmosphere at about one hundred six thousand miles per hour. It slowed so violently that it felt more than two hundred times the pull of Earth's gravity.

The gas in front of its heat shield blazed about as hot as the surface of the Sun. So let's assume you survive the plunge and open a parachute high above the clouds.

Up here, it's brutally cold. At the level where the pressure matches sea level on Earth, the temperature is about minus one hundred sixty degrees Fahrenheit. The air is mostly hydrogen, with traces of methane and ammonia. None of it is breathable.

As you drift down, you pass through decks of clouds. Scientists think the top layer is ammonia ice, the middle is crystals of ammonium hydrosulfide, and the deepest is water ice and vapor.

Then the sky flashes. Juno spotted shallow lightning up here, sparked in clouds of ammonia mixed with water. On Earth, lightning comes from water clouds. On Jupiter, the ammonia acts like antifreeze, keeping droplets liquid where they would otherwise freeze.

Those storms may also build strange hailstones that scientists nicknamed mushballs: layers of ammonia water slush and ice, wrapped in a crust of water ice. Once heavy enough, they plunge deep, melt, and vanish, dragging ammonia down with them.

Meanwhile, your own body feels wrong. Gravity at Jupiter's cloud tops is about two point four times what you feel on Earth. If you weigh one hundred fifty pounds, here you'd weigh about three hundred fifty five. Lifting your arms would feel like hauling sandbags.

And you would not hang in one place. Jupiter spins once every ten hours, the shortest day of any planet. Winds near the equator can reach about three hundred thirty five miles per hour, faster than any hurricane on Earth.

Before you sink deeper, glance up. Jupiter has one hundred fifteen officially recognized moons. The four biggest, Io, Europa, Ganymede, and Callisto, were discovered by Galileo Galilei in sixteen ten. Galileo's probe was named after him.

Off to one side churns the Great Red Spot, a storm wider than our entire planet. Astronomers have watched it for more than three hundred years, and Juno found that its roots plunge hundreds of miles below the cloud tops.

But that famous storm is shrinking. In the late eighteen hundreds, it stretched about twenty five thousand miles across. By 2014, Hubble measured it at roughly ten thousand. Why it's shrinking is still unexplained, and its future is up for debate.

So you sink, and there's still no floor. The gas just gets thicker and hotter around you. Galileo's probe kept radioing data for about fifty eight minutes, falling more than ninety miles into the clouds.

When it finally went silent, the pressure was about twenty three times what you feel at sea level, and the temperature had climbed above three hundred degrees Fahrenheit. That pressure is like being about seven hundred feet under the ocean.

There is no moment where you hit an ocean. The hydrogen around you is squeezed until it behaves like a liquid, but there's no clear line where gas ends. You never splash. You just end up swimming in what used to be sky.

Keep going, and things turn truly alien. Far below, the pressure squeezes hydrogen so hard that electrons are pushed off its atoms, and it conducts electricity like a metal. This deep ocean of metallic hydrogen is thought to help power Jupiter's gigantic magnetic field.

And down here, it might be raining. Galileo's probe found too little helium and neon in Jupiter's upper air. Computer models suggest helium separates into droplets that sink toward the center, carrying the neon with them. Helium rain, falling inside a planet.

And at the very center? For decades, scientists pictured a small, solid core. But Juno found that Jupiter's core is much larger than expected, and partly dissolved, blending gradually into the hydrogen around it. Scientists call it fuzzy.

How did it get that way? A 2019 study proposed young Jupiter was hit head on by a newborn planet ten times Earth's mass. A 2025 study argued an impact can't do it, and the fuzziness formed as Jupiter grew. Nobody knows yet.

And leaving? To escape Jupiter's gravity from the cloud tops, you'd need about one hundred thirty thousand miles per hour, more than five times what it takes to leave Earth. That would take a rocket far beyond anything humans have built.

Even our robots don't get out. In 2003, with its fuel running out, NASA steered the Galileo orbiter straight into Jupiter on purpose, so it could never crash into Europa and contaminate a moon that may hide an ocean beneath its ice.

So you can never stand on Jupiter. You can only fall through it, deeper, hotter, and more crushed, until there's nothing left of you. Jupiter isn't a world you land on. It's a world you sink into.

Your immune system can remember a germ it fought years ago. A vaccine lets it practice that fight without the danger. But how?

To understand vaccines, you first need to understand the problem they solve. So let's start with what happens when a brand new germ gets into your body, one your immune system has never met before.

Your first line of defense responds quickly, but its cells are generalists. They attack anything that looks foreign. Against a new germ, your body needs a more precise weapon, and building it takes time.

Your immune cells are made in your bone marrow. Most of the time, only small numbers of each kind patrol your body. When a threat appears, the right ones multiply into an army, and then shrink back once it's over.

Every germ carries molecules on its surface that your immune system can recognize, called antigens. Think of an antigen as the germ's face, a unique feature that gives away exactly who it is.

Special cells called antigen presenting cells grab the invader, break it apart, and display small pieces of it on their surface, like holding up a wanted poster for the rest of your immune system to see.

Helper T cells read those wanted posters. When one finds a match, it switches on and sends out chemical signals that call other immune cells into the fight. It's like a commander sounding the alarm.

B cells answer the call. The ones that match the antigen multiply and become specialists, pumping out antibodies, Y shaped proteins that latch on to the germ and help neutralize it.

Killer T cells join in too. Their job is to find your own cells that the germ has already infected and destroy them, so the germ can't use them to make more copies of itself.

Here's the catch. Building that precise army from scratch can take days or even weeks. Meanwhile, the germ keeps multiplying. That race is why a first infection can sometimes make you very sick.

Once the infection is cleared, most of those specialist cells die off. But a small number stay behind as memory cells. They are veterans that remember exactly what that germ looks like.

If the same germ ever shows up again, the memory cells recognize it fast and launch a quicker, stronger response, often stopping the germ before it can cause disease.

This is immunity. For many diseases, it's why you're protected after getting sick once. But earning that memory the natural way has a price. You have to get through the illness first.

There's also a borrowed kind of protection, called passive immunity. A baby, for example, receives ready made antibodies from its mother in the last months of pregnancy. It works right away, but fades within weeks or months.

Active immunity, the kind your own body builds after an infection or a vaccine, takes longer to develop. But it lasts much longer, because your immune system made it, and it remembers.

A vaccine offers a shortcut. The CDC describes vaccines as imitating an infection. They show your immune system the germ's antigens, the wanted poster, without the dangers of the real disease.

Your immune system responds much as it would to the real thing. Antigen presenting cells display the pieces, helper T cells sound the alarm, B cells make antibodies, and crucially, memory cells are left behind.

It's a bit like a fire drill. Everyone learns the escape route on a calm, ordinary day, so that if a real fire ever comes, nobody has to figure it out in a panic.

Scientists have found several ways to show your immune system that wanted poster. According to the CDC, a vaccine's antigen may be a weakened germ, a killed germ, pieces of a germ, or a toxin made harmless.

Live weakened vaccines use a germ that has been changed so it causes little or no illness. Because it's so close to the real thing, it can give long lasting protection, often after two doses.

But because the germ is still alive, the CDC notes these vaccines aren't suitable for people with seriously weakened immune systems. For them, non live vaccines are the safer option.

Non live vaccines, made from killed germs or just pieces of them, can't cause the infection. They often need several doses, and their protection can fade over time, which is why booster shots exist.

Some bacteria, like the ones behind tetanus, harm us mainly through a toxin they release. Vaccines against them use a version of the toxin that's been made harmless, so your body learns to neutralize the poison itself.

Some germs keep changing their faces. Flu viruses change so often that, as the CDC notes, the seasonal flu vaccine is updated regularly, giving your immune system an up to date wanted poster.

A newer approach uses messenger RNA. Instead of delivering the antigen itself, it delivers instructions. The CDC explains that these vaccines teach your cells how to make a harmless piece of protein.

Your cells read the instructions, build that protein piece, and your immune system responds to it. According to the CDC, the vaccine material doesn't enter the cell's nucleus, where your DNA is kept, and it's broken down afterward.

Whatever the type, training takes time. According to the CDC, it typically takes a few weeks after vaccination for the body to produce the T cells and B cells it needs. Protection isn't instant.

Many people feel tired, a little sore or mildly feverish for a day or two. The CDC describes symptoms like these as normal signs that the body is building immunity, your immune system busy at work.

No vaccine is perfect, and WHO notes that none is one hundred percent effective. But according to the CDC, vaccinated people who do get infected are far less likely to become seriously ill.

Vaccines also work at the level of a whole community. When enough people are immune, a germ has a harder time spreading. WHO calls this herd immunity, and it helps protect people who can't be vaccinated.

That includes people with certain health conditions that weaken their immune systems. Still, WHO notes that herd immunity doesn't give them full protection, so it works best alongside other care.

The idea behind all of this is older than you might think. In 1796, an English doctor named Edward Jenner noticed that milkmaids who had caught mild cowpox seemed protected from smallpox, a deadly disease.

Jenner took material from a cowpox sore and gave it to an eight year old boy named James Phipps. Months later, the boy was exposed to smallpox several times, and he never got sick.

Nearly two centuries later, after a huge worldwide vaccination effort, the world was declared free of smallpox in 1980. Many people consider it the biggest achievement in international public health.

Which vaccines are right for you depends on your age, your health and your history. Those decisions are best made with a doctor or other healthcare provider, using official guidance.

So a vaccine isn't really a shield you carry around. It's a lesson. It teaches your own immune cells to recognize a germ, and then your body keeps that memory, ready for the day it might matter.

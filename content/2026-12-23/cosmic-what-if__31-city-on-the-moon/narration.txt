What if we stopped visiting the Moon, and built a real city there, where you could live?

This is not pure fantasy anymore. In April 2026, the four astronauts of Artemis Two flew around the Moon and came home, traveling farther from Earth than any humans before them. The next goal is to stay.

So let's plan your city, step by step. Step one: location. The Moon is about two hundred thirty-nine thousand miles away. A radio message from home takes a little over one second to reach you.

That short delay matters. You could video call your family almost in real time. And the trip is short by space standards: the whole Artemis Two flight, out around the Moon and back, took about ten days.

Most of the Moon is a terrible place to settle. Its day, from one sunrise to the next, lasts about twenty-nine and a half Earth days. That means roughly two weeks of sunlight, then two weeks of darkness.

And with almost no air to spread heat around, the temperature swings are brutal. NASA says the sunlit surface reaches about two hundred sixty degrees Fahrenheit. In darkness, it drops to about minus two hundred eighty.

So planners look at the south pole. The Moon's axis is barely tilted, so near the pole the Sun skims along the horizon. High crater rims there can be sunlit about ninety percent of the time.

Nearly constant sunlight means nearly constant solar power. That alone makes those ridges some of the most valuable real estate in the solar system. But the south pole has a second, even bigger prize.

Right next to those bright ridges are craters whose floors never see the Sun at all. They are among the coldest places ever measured in the solar system, and NASA says they hold high concentrations of water ice.

Water is the key to your whole city. You can drink it. You can grow food with it. And split apart with electricity, it becomes hydrogen and oxygen, which is air to breathe and rocket fuel.

Step two: building materials. You cannot ship a city from Earth. Every brick would have to ride a rocket. So you build with what is lying around, and the Moon is covered in it.

It is called regolith: a charcoal gray blanket of powdery dust and broken rock that covers nearly the entire Moon. To a builder, it is an endless supply of free raw material.

Back in 2013, the European Space Agency worked with architects on a design where robots three-D print a protective shell out of lunar soil, layer by layer, over an inflatable dome.

The idea is to land a compact module, inflate a dome, and let the printer bury it under a thick shell of regolith with a hollow, foam-like structure inside, strong but light, like a bird's bone.

In their tests, they mixed simulated moon soil with magnesium oxide and a binding salt, which turned it into a stone-like solid. Your walls would literally be made of the Moon.

Their printer could build at around two meters per hour, and the team hoped a next generation machine would go even faster. The dream is that robots could have shelters waiting for you before the first residents ever arrive.

Step three: air. And this is where moon dust gets really surprising. By weight, roughly forty to forty-five percent of lunar regolith is oxygen. It is just locked inside minerals and glass.

You will need every bit of it, because outside there is nothing to breathe. NASA describes the Moon's atmosphere as a very thin, tenuous exosphere. Even at noon, the sky above your city is black.

In the lab, ESA engineers placed simulated regolith in molten salt and ran an electric current through it. In about fifty hours, they pulled out ninety-six percent of the oxygen.

Even the leftovers are useful. What remains is a mix of metal alloys, which could become tools, parts, or building material. In a moon city, nothing goes to waste.

Step four: staying alive. The Moon has no thick atmosphere and no global magnetic field. Nothing stops the radiation streaming through space. In 2019, China's Chang'e Four lander measured it.

The result: on the lunar surface, the dose is roughly two hundred times higher than on Earth's surface, and roughly two and a half times higher than astronauts get inside the International Space Station.

So your city cannot just sit on the surface. You need a thick roof of rock or soil over your head. And the Moon may already have built one for you.

Billions of years ago, rivers of lava flowed across the Moon. When the outside cooled and the inside drained away, some left long empty tunnels called lava tubes.

In 2024, scientists reanalyzed radar data from NASA's Lunar Reconnaissance Orbiter. Beneath a pit in the Sea of Tranquility, roughly a hundred meters wide, they found evidence of a cave passage leading underground.

The team said the most likely explanation is an empty lava tube. A natural tunnel like that could protect you from radiation, tiny meteorites, and those wild temperature swings. Your city might be underground.

Step five: life in the city. Walk outside, and you weigh about one sixth of what you weigh on Earth. Every step becomes a bounce. Scientists still do not know what years in low gravity would do to your body.

Then there is the dust. It is abrasive and clings to everything. After Apollo Seventeen, astronaut Harrison Schmitt described breathing it as lunar hay fever: sneezing, watery eyes, and a sore throat.

So your city will be obsessed with keeping dust out. Every airlock, every suit, every seal is a battle against grains sharp enough to scratch surfaces and wear through fabric.

Now look up. Because the Moon keeps the same face toward Earth, our planet does not rise and set the way the Moon does for us. From the near side, Earth hangs in roughly the same part of your sky.

It goes through phases, just like the Moon does from here. On a full Earth night, the blue planet lights up your gray streets. Every person you have ever known is up there, in one glowing ball.

And here is the strangest part of living on the Moon. Your clocks would not agree with clocks on Earth. Not because they are broken, but because of Einstein.

Weaker gravity makes time run slightly faster. In 2024, researchers at the National Institute of Standards and Technology calculated that clocks on the Moon gain about fifty-six millionths of a second every day.

That sounds tiny. But navigation systems depend on perfect timing. Without correcting for it, a lunar GPS would slowly drift off. So scientists are working on a standard Moon time, a time zone for an entire world.

Put it all together. A city at the south pole, powered by sunlight on the ridges, drinking ice from the shadows, breathing oxygen baked out of dust, sheltered in tunnels of ancient lava.

None of that requires new physics. Every piece has been studied, measured, or tested. The hard part is doing all of it at once, a quarter of a million miles from the nearest hardware store.

So, what if we built a city on the Moon? You would bounce down the street, hide from the sky, and watch Earth go through its phases. And your watch would run just a little bit fast.
